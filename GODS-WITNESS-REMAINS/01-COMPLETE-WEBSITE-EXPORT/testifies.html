Why this title?

An archive ordinarily presents evidence about the world outside itself. This work proposes a more unusual approach: examining the archive’s own records, descriptions, histories, versions and integrity checks as evidence that can also be tested.

The archive becomes both the object of investigation and the instrument through which the investigation is conducted.

Its documents are connected to their identifiable sources. Its claims are linked to the records offered in support. Its historical manifests are compared with later inventories.

This is not a presumption that the archive proves its own allegations. It is a commitment to making every material assertion open to independent scrutiny.

The value of the archive lies in making these distinctions visible—and allowing readers to follow the evidence themselves.

The ultimate test is not whether the archive asks to be believed. It is whether another person can reproduce its checks, inspect its sources, challenge its interpretations and reach an independently reasoned conclusion.

PROLOGUE — WHAT IF THE ARCHIVE IS ALSO A DOCUMENT?

Most people read an archive from beginning to end. They open a PDF, read its pages, close it and move to the next file.

That is only the first dimension.

An archive can also be read sideways: across copies, dates, filenames, file hashes, repository histories, official correspondence, website references and the records that describe how the archive itself was assembled.

A letter may be one document. A copy of that letter in another repository is a second location. A checksum catalogue describing the file is a third record. A website page discussing it is a fourth. A later document responding to it may form a fifth connection.

Together, these records can reveal something that none of them necessarily reveals alone: the history of how a document travelled, how it was described, whether its digital contents remained consistent, and what remains to be verified.

This is the idea behind The Evidence Constellation.

It does not presume that every connection proves misconduct. It asks a more exacting question: What can be established when every document is connected to its source, its copies, its provenance records, its public presentation and the questions that remain unresolved?

I. THE FIVE LAYERS OF THE CONSTELLATION

Layer 1 — The primary document This is the object being examined: a letter, decision, report, email, photograph, recording, manuscript or other original record.

The first question is not what the filename claims. It is what the document itself says.

Layer 2 — The source of authority Who created or issued it? Is the issuing body identified? Does the document contain a date, reference number, signature, letterhead or other identifying information? Can its authenticity be checked against an authoritative source?

A document stored in a repository is not automatically authenticated by that repository.

Layer 3 — The digital identity What is the exact repository path? What are the file size and SHA-256 checksum? Does another copy have the same checksum? Is the file a genuine PDF, a placeholder, a Git Large File Storage pointer or a different object entirely?

This layer helps distinguish a document’s digital identity from its title. ● ● ● ● ● Layer 4 — The public narrative Where is the document discussed on the website? Which essays, statements, indexes or publications refer to it? Are those references accurate representations of the source, or interpretations authored by the archive’s owner?

This distinction allows a reader to move between evidence and commentary without confusing the two.

Layer 5 — The unresolved question What does the record establish? What does it not establish? Is there a missing response, a contradictory date, an unexplained version difference or a connection that still needs independent confirmation?

An honest archive does not hide the fifth layer. It makes uncertainty navigable. ⸻

IV. THREE DIGITAL FORENSIC CASE STUDIES

These examples demonstrate why the connections matter.

Case study A — Two independently matching official-document checksums Document 1: IBAC outcome letter, 9 April 2020 Document 2: OAIC privacy document, March 2021 In the earlier integrity test, both files were retrieved as PDF content and their calculated SHA-256 checksums matched the corresponding catalogue entries.

What is established: the retrieved bytes matched the recorded hashes for these two checks. Both files also began with a PDF header.

What is not established by that result alone: that every statement in either document is true, that the issuing body’s records are complete, or that the archive contains every relevant related document.

Why it matters: an independently repeatable integrity check turns a general statement that “the document is preserved” into a specific, testable result.

Case study B — A checksum discrepancy Document: “THE DIGITAL APOCALYPSE OF ADMINISTRATIVE ANNIHILATION”

The catalogue recorded a size of 105,971 bytes. The retrieved file was 104,980 bytes. The recorded checksum was: 5e593e652a6f06b6c1aa9fc6dae562031cea6c1132c9571 73522a4fb7d337c9b The checksum calculated from the retrieved bytes was: 1. c1628e146002f9fefb3e10dc80f2d2345ba667767759d30 855edb1ed57b4f4dd The retrieved file began with a valid PDF header, but its calculated hash did not match the catalogue.
