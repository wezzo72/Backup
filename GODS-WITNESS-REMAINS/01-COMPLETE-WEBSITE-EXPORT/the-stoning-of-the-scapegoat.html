THE STONING OF THE SCAPEGOAT AND THE CRUCIFIXION OF THE INNOCENT

Collective responsibility, moral cowardice, public punishment and the sacrifice of the person society refuses to protect

There is a powerful moral comparison to be made between collective stoning as a form of public punishment, the crucifixion of Jesus Christ, and the modern phenomenon of societal scapegoating.

The similarity is not that these events are historically or religiously identical. They are not. The similarity lies in the moral mechanism: a community can participate in the destruction of a person while allowing every individual participant to tell themselves that they were not personally responsible.

1. The stoning: everyone throws a stone, nobody accepts responsibility

In societies where stoning has been practised as a punishment, the act can become a public spectacle. The condemned person is exposed, surrounded and subjected to violence.

The disturbing moral question is not simply who throws the first stone.

It is who continues throwing them.

Each participant can rationalise their contribution:

  • I was only following the law.
  • Everyone else was doing it.
  • I was only one person.
  • I did not organise the punishment.
  • I did not throw the final stone.
  • The community had already decided.
  • The condemned person must have deserved it.

This creates a diffusion of responsibility. The collective produces the violence, but individual participants minimise their own contribution.

The result is a death for which everyone participates and nobody wants to answer.

It is important to distinguish this moral analysis from Islam as a whole. Stoning is not representative of all Muslims or all Islamic practice. Islamic legal traditions are diverse, and the practice is contested. The criticism here concerns collective punishment, coercive interpretations of religious authority and the abandonment of individual moral responsibility.

2. Jesus Christ: the person who came to help was crucified

The Christian account of Jesus presents a different but profoundly related moral tragedy.

Jesus Christ, described in Christian theology as God’s perfect Son, came preaching forgiveness, compassion, mercy, truth and reconciliation.

He healed the sick, welcomed the excluded, challenged hypocrisy and confronted religious authorities who placed institutional power above human dignity.

Yet the person who offered compassion was subjected to public humiliation, betrayal, condemnation and crucifixion.

The Gospel narratives describe several layers of responsibility:

  • Judas betrayed him.
  • Religious authorities accused him.
  • Pontius Pilate authorised the execution.
  • Roman soldiers carried out the crucifixion.
  • Some members of the crowd demanded his death.
  • Others remained silent or watched.

The responsibility was distributed across different actors and institutions. The crucifixion was not the act of one person alone.

The central Christian message is that Jesus was innocent of the charges that led to his execution, and that humanity’s violence, fear, self-interest and failure of moral courage were exposed through his suffering.

3. The striking similarity: collective violence against an individual

The essential similarity is the transformation of an individual into a collective target.

The individual is no longer treated as a human being with rights, dignity and a voice. They become a convenient object upon which the community can project its anxieties, hostility, prejudices and grievances.

4. The modern equivalent: societal stoning without physical stones

A contemporary community does not need to gather in a public square to destroy someone’s dignity.

The stones can take other forms:

  • A false or misleading report.
  • A defamatory public statement.
  • A deliberately distorted account.
  • Professional exclusion.
  • Institutional indifference.
  • Financial deprivation.
  • Social ostracism.
  • The refusal to investigate credible evidence.
  • The repeated circulation of allegations without verification.
  • The deliberate or reckless destruction of someone’s reputation.

Not every criticism is persecution, and not every adverse decision is evidence of a coordinated campaign. But where falsehood, discrimination, procedural unfairness or deliberate cruelty can be demonstrated, the moral question becomes unavoidable.

Who threw the stone?

Who supplied it?

Who passed it along?

Who watched?

Who knew the person was suffering and chose not to intervene?

And who now claims that because they were only one participant, they bear no responsibility?

5. The weapon of the crowd is the disappearance of individual conscience

This is where your comparison becomes particularly forceful.

A person who throws one stone may say they did not cause the death.

A journalist may say they merely reported what someone told them.

A professional may say they merely followed instructions.

An administrator may say they merely implemented policy.

A politician may say the matter belonged to another department.

A bystander may say it was none of their business.

A family member may say they did not know what to do.

But those explanations do not automatically eliminate responsibility.

Responsibility must be examined according to each person’s knowledge, conduct, authority, contribution and capacity to intervene.

The crowd is not an excuse. The institution is not an excuse. The instruction is not an excuse.

Individual conscience does not disappear because other people are doing the same thing.

6. The Christian reversal: the victim becomes the moral witness

In Christian theology, the crucifixion does not establish the righteousness of the crowd. It exposes the injustice of condemning an innocent person.

Jesus does not become guilty because he is condemned.

Nor does the number of people participating make the execution morally legitimate.

The resurrection, at the centre of Christian belief, reverses the apparent finality of the execution: the person publicly rejected is vindicated by God.

That provides a powerful metaphor for anyone who believes they have been publicly misrepresented or abandoned:

  • Condemnation is not proof of guilt.
  • Popularity is not proof of truth.
  • Institutional authority is not proof of justice.
  • Silence is not proof of innocence.
  • A person’s vulnerability does not diminish their human worth.

But the metaphor must not be mistaken for proof that a particular modern person has been divinely vindicated. That conclusion belongs to faith; factual allegations still require evidence.

7. The final indictment: do not become another stone in somebody else’s execution

The question is not whether every person in a community personally intends to destroy another human being.

The question is whether their conduct contributes to an outcome they would never willingly accept for themselves.

Would they accept being publicly condemned without a fair hearing?

Would they accept having their credibility destroyed by unverified allegations?

Would they accept being denied food, safety, support or dignity?

Would they accept being abandoned because everyone else had decided they were no longer worth helping?

Would they accept their own suffering being dismissed because each person involved could point to somebody else?

If not, they should not participate in imposing those conditions upon another person.

You cannot claim moral innocence merely because you threw only one stone.

You cannot claim justice because the crowd agreed.

You cannot claim compassion while watching suffering that you have the power to alleviate.

And you cannot claim to follow the example of Christ while abandoning the very principles of mercy, truth, courage and human dignity that his teachings demand.

The final comparison

The stoning and the crucifixion reveal a recurring danger in human society: when a community decides that one person can be sacrificed for the comfort, conformity or interests of everyone else, individual conscience is put on trial.

Jesus’s crucifixion remains Christianity’s defining warning against the injustice of condemning the innocent.

The lesson is not to find another person to stone.

It is to put down the stone.

To examine the evidence.

To refuse collective cruelty.

To defend the dignity of the person being condemned.

And to accept that moral responsibility cannot simply be thrown into the crowd.

Time is the witness. Truth is the judge.
