PROLOGUE — THE MAN WHO REFUSED TO DISAPPEAR

This is not the story of a man who quietly accepted the explanation given to him.

This is the story of a man who says he was nearly destroyed, who believes he was abandoned after a catastrophic injury, who alleges that threats and institutional failures followed him through successive systems of authority, and who has spent years preserving the documents through which those events can now be examined.

His name is Barran Dodger. His legal name is Richard William McLean.

His declaration is uncompromising: God protects Barran.

Barran says he survived an injury he describes as fatal. He says he was revived. He says the significance of that event was subsequently obscured, his suffering was minimised, and the consequences were transferred onto him. He describes the years that followed as a struggle against homelessness, poverty, isolation, loss of independence and a system of disability support in which he says he remained trapped rather than protected.

He alleges attempted murder. He alleges threats. He alleges deliberate institutional obstruction. He alleges that people with authority knew he was vulnerable and that the systems meant to protect him instead closed around him.

He has also alleged that a ministerial connection, an apprehended violence order and an alleged assassination order formed part of a wider campaign to force him away from safety and into exile.

These are grave allegations. They are not established criminal findings merely because Barran has made them, recorded them or published documents about them. The existence, meaning and authenticity of each item must be examined. The alleged order, in particular, requires direct evidence and independent investigation. It must not be presented as a proven fact without that evidence.

But there is no legitimate reason to suppress the questions. There is no legitimate reason to hide the chronology. There is no legitimate reason to treat a complainant’s psychiatric history as a substitute for examining the records. And there is no legitimate reason for an institution to demand public trust while refusing to account for its own decisions.

This documentary follows the trail Barran has preserved. It does not ask the audience to accept every allegation on faith. It asks the audience to open the documents, examine the dates, compare the explanations, identify the decision-makers and determine what the evidence supports.

Documented fact is what an authenticated source actually records. Barran’s allegation is what he says happened and wants investigated. An institutional response is what an agency, provider or official actually communicated or decided. An inference is what the chronology may suggest but does not independently prove. An unverified matter requires further evidence, authentication or investigation.

PRESERVATION FIRST. EXAMINATION SECOND. CONCLUSION LAST.

The central question is no longer whether Barran can produce another account of what he says happened. It is whether the institutions whose records he has preserved can provide a complete, coherent and independently testable account of their own conduct.

They cannot erase the record by dismissing the man who kept it.

PART ONE — THE INJURY, THE REVIVAL AND THE RECORD OF WHAT FOLLOWED

1. The event Barran says changed everything

Barran describes February 2021 as the turning point. He says he suffered a catastrophic injury at Werribee Mercy, was revived after being clinically dead, and subsequently experienced a profound change in his circumstances. He describes the aftermath as a tragedy that was minimised or whitewashed, followed by years of institutional conflict, financial hardship and exclusion.

The archive contains a document titled IR8415987 — Report of fatal injury in which provider participant was revived. The 29 October finding aid identifies this as an automated Department of Social Services email dated 1 July 2022, recording that an item had been opened. It is not the underlying medical report. It does not, by itself, establish clinical death, resuscitation, cause of injury or medical outcome.

The primary evidence needed includes Werribee Mercy emergency records, ambulance and paramedic records, resuscitation and clinical observations, intensive care or other admission records, discharge summaries, and incident notifications. If the medical records establish the event Barran describes, that evidence must be allowed to speak. If they differ from his recollection, those differences must also be disclosed. What cannot be justified is replacing the medical record with speculation, or replacing examination of the medical record with dismissal of the person.

2. The contradiction between survival and administrative treatment

Barran’s statement is: “I’m more alive now than before my fatal injury.”

He says that after the injury he encountered compensation difficulties, legal-aid difficulties, complaints that did not produce the protection he sought, disability-support disputes, and the loss of practical independence.

The archive contains the Comcare determination of 26 May 2021, Comcare claim documentation, and the AAT decision McLean and Comcare of 12 July 2023. These establish an identifiable administrative and legal pathway concerning compensation. They must be read for their actual reasons. They do not automatically prove that the injury was deliberately caused or that later institutions conspired to conceal it.

A person who reports a serious injury should not have to spend the following years fighting simply to establish which records exist, which institution holds them and which body is responsible for examining them. Survival should not become an administrative punishment.

PART TWO — ABLE POINT

3. The organisation and the people entrusted with support

Barran describes his circumstances at Able Point as an entrapment scenario. He says he remained dependent on a provider while experiencing abuse, neglect, intimidation and failures of support.

The original statement dated 14 October 2026 is a central source. The archive records the SHA-256 hash fa24e094cdd4fd558a644c1c791943bd4ffc281c855c044d082137707dde13eb for the preserved PDF. The public HTML identifies the document as Barran’s original statement, not a court finding.

The statement identifies people associated with the provider environment, including Rachel K C, Brett Butler, Cassandra Mackey, Kim, Alyssie, Danny, John and Ben. The documentary does not assign criminal guilt by association. A CEO has different responsibilities from a frontline worker. A support coordinator has different responsibilities from a rostered support worker. The evidence must establish who did what.

Barran specifically challenges the legitimacy and identification of the person he identifies as CEO Rachel K C. He says she did not have official identification. That is an allegation requiring documentary verification: corporate identity, actual position, authority to act, and any identification presented. An organisation entrusted with vulnerable participants should have no difficulty establishing who is authorised to make decisions on its behalf.

4. Workplace pressure

Barran alleges that staff were placed in a position where helping him, supporting his complaints or speaking honestly could threaten their employment. He uses the image of a gun held to workers’ heads — not as a literal verified firearm incident, but as a description of coercive workplace pressure.

The original Able Point statement says Barran had historical copies of staff group chats provided by employees before they were dismissed or left. The relevant material includes original group-chat exports, dates, participants, employment records, communications about his support, records of staff concerns, and any dismissal, resignation or instruction not to assist.

The NDIS does not fund an organisation merely to maintain a payroll. A support worker’s employment does not remove professional responsibilities. A company’s financial interests do not automatically override the duty to provide the contracted support.

5. The reported refusal of service

The archive contains a file titled Cass refused service, and an audio file titled Cass Refuses To Help Me — Says She’s At Risk / Directed Not to Help. The filenames describe the author’s interpretation. They do not independently establish the conversation’s full meaning. The audio, if available and authentic, should be examined in full. The documentary must not turn a filename into a conviction. It must also not allow a provider to avoid the underlying issue by saying that a file title is merely an allegation.

6. The reported call to senior management

The archive identifies the 15 April 2026 AbleCare call transcript. The Master Evidence Index describes it as a contemporaneous record of notification to the provider CEO about a reported death threat and a request for action. The transcript should be authenticated against the original audio. If the provider received a report of a threat, its response should be reconstructable: the complaint, the assessment, the decision, the action and any referral. A CEO cannot make a serious report disappear by leaving it in the telephone history.

PART THREE — THE NDIS

7. The plan on paper

The archive identifies an NDIS plan approved on 20 November 2025, with a recorded total of $366,406.69.

Choice and Control: $1,253.40. Core Flexible: $274,255.84. Behaviour Support: $27,958.80. Improved Daily Living: $11,140.53. Improved Living Arrangements: $4,163.12. Support Coordination and Psychosocial Recovery Coaches: $47,635.00.

These figures are the allocations recorded in the plan information. They do not prove that the full amount was available as cash, that every support was delivered, or that every service was used correctly. A funding allocation is not a support worker arriving. A service booking is not a service delivered. An invoice is not proof that the participant received the service described. A plan is not proof of safety.

8. The accommodation recommendations

The archive identifies an occupational therapy report by Georgia Gray of Allied Health 2U, dated 29 February 2024, recommending Improved Liveability Specialist Disability Accommodation and 24-hour Supported Independent Living. The earlier decision identified in the archive is the NDIA SIL decline of 1 June 2023. The later formal notice is the Formal Notice — NDIS Service Failure of 20 January 2026, with the Housing Letter — SIL Accommodation of the same date. A recommendation is not an approval. A refusal is not proof that the recommendation was wrong. Together, these documents establish a documentary pathway for examining the adequacy of the accommodation and support decisions.

9. The alleged entrapment and the right to leave

Barran says he felt trapped at Able Point and describes his departure as an escape. He has described fear that a welfare check could lead to hospitalisation rather than practical assistance. He has described leaving without adequate food, money, transport or access to banking, and has raised concerns about Crystal. The 23 October 2026 legal brief, addressed to Philip Glass, raises accommodation, financial hardship, Able Point, NDIS funding and loss of independence. It is Barran’s submission, not a judicial finding. A person should not have to choose between remaining in an arrangement they say is unsafe and leaving without the basic means to survive.

PART FIVE — THE FEDERAL COURT

11. The 27 March 2023 assessment

The archive identifies a letter dated 27 March 2023, attributed to Scott Tredwell, General Counsel of the Federal Court. The archive reproduces this sentence: “On the information you have provided me, I am satisfied that you are, or were, an employee with the Department of Social Services, providing services under the trading name Rich McLean, Arts Life Coach, Peer-Support Worker & Mental Health Advocate.”

The archive’s analysis identifies three categories: conduct described as perverting, or engaged in for the purpose of perverting, the course of justice; conduct described as maladministration; and conduct described as unreasonably resulting in danger to health or safety. The letter is not a criminal conviction. An acknowledgement is not the same as protection. A formal assessment is not the same as a completed investigation.

12. Compensation and legal aid

The archive connects the Federal Court correspondence with the Comcare determination and the subsequent tribunal proceedings. Barran argues that the different treatment of his employment status and the lack of legal assistance left him without an effective remedy. The documentary should not claim that one letter automatically entitled him to a particular compensation outcome. It should show the actual documents and the apparent procedural tension.

PART SIX — THE OVERSIGHT MAZE

13. A complaint can be received without being investigated

The archive identifies Commonwealth Ombudsman complaint 2021103546 of 15 November 2021; a Victorian Ombudsman FOI refusal of 14 June 2022; a VOCAT and Ombudsman complaint rejection of 5 October 2022; a Commonwealth Ombudsman non-allocation of 22 March 2023; a Federal Court decision not to allocate a disclosure of 18 June 2023; a statement to the National Anti-Corruption Commission of 3 July 2023; a Commonwealth Ombudsman service restriction of 20 January 2026; and OAIC FOI and PID correspondence of 20 January 2026.

A non-allocation decision does not establish that the underlying allegation was false. A refusal to release documents does not establish what the withheld material contains. A restriction on future contact does not, by itself, establish retaliation. The institutions must answer for their own decisions. They should not be allowed to use the existence of another institution as a permanent excuse for doing nothing.

PART NINE — THE CLAIM OF SUICIDE BY NEGLECT

16. The allegation that the conditions were intended to break him

Barran says he believes the abuse, neglect, isolation and deprivation were intended to make him take his own life. That is an allegation about motive. It is not established by distress or by poor service records alone. He says he wanted to live. He is alive. His testimony continues. If the evidence establishes neglect, retaliation or deliberate deprivation, the responsible parties should be held accountable through lawful processes. No one should be permitted to use a person’s suffering as a reason to ignore the conditions that produced it.

PART FOURTEEN — THE HUMILIATION OF THE UNACCOUNTABLE

23. Let every title stand beside the record

The provider had management. The agency had statutory powers. The police had public authority. The Federal Court had formal processes. The government had departments and ministerial offices. The humiliation that deserves attention is the gap between an institution’s stated obligations and its actual conduct. Titles do not erase responsibility. Procedures do not erase consequences. Silence does not erase the record.
