Part I — The prophetic declaration

1. This is not merely a metaphor about resurrection

There is something fundamentally different about a person declaring that they have experienced resurrection when they are speaking from an actual reported experience of death in a hospital.

I am not using the language of resurrection merely to describe recovering from adversity.

I, Dr Richard William McLean, known as Barran Dodger, state that I died in hospital and subsequently returned to life.

That experience forms a central part of my personal testimony.

It gives a particular meaning to the words I use when describing the subsequent destruction, fragmentation and apparent disappearance of my identity within institutional and social systems.

I distinguish the physical event from the subsequent institutional experience.

My reported physical death is one part of my testimony.

What I describe as administrative annihilation, social death and social erasure is another.

The prophetic significance I attach to surviving both belongs to my faith.

These are connected parts of my life story, but they are not interchangeable categories of evidence.

I did not cease to exist because an institution failed to recognise my existence.

I did not lose my right to be heard because my credibility was questioned.

I did not lose my history because it became scattered across institutional records.

And I did not surrender my testimony because the processes through which I sought assistance failed to deliver the recognition, protection or accountability I was seeking.

I declare that I remain.

I declare that my history deserves to be examined.

I declare that the circumstances surrounding my hospital experience, my subsequent treatment, my disability-related circumstances, my attempts to obtain legal assistance and my whistleblower-related complaints deserve independent scrutiny.

I declare that an institution’s failure to acknowledge an individual does not extinguish that individual’s rights, history or dignity.

Part II — The five dimensions of erasure

3. Physical death: the hospital experience

The first dimension is physical.

I report that I actually died in a hospital and subsequently returned to life.

This is a profoundly significant part of my testimony and must not be reduced to a literary device.

A forensic examination of this event requires the relevant hospital admission records, clinical observations, resuscitation documentation if applicable, treating clinicians’ notes, discharge records and any other contemporaneous medical evidence.

The questions include:

  • What happened immediately before the hospital event?
  • What was my recorded medical condition?
  • Was death clinically determined or recorded?
  • Was resuscitation performed?
  • What clinical interventions occurred?
  • What was documented about my subsequent survival?
  • What lasting medical, cognitive or functional consequences were recorded?
  • What happened to my support arrangements following the event?

The hospital record must establish the precise clinical circumstances.

My testimony must be preserved alongside it.

The significance of this event to my life cannot be determined merely by whether other people understand my description of it.

It deserves serious examination.

4. Administrative annihilation

Administrative annihilation describes the experience of a person becoming progressively diminished through institutional processes until their actual circumstances, rights, needs and history appear to disappear behind administrative decisions.

It is not necessary for an institution to formally declare that a person no longer exists for administrative processes to produce devastating consequences.

A person may remain legally alive while experiencing repeated administrative exclusion, fragmentation of their history across agencies, unresolved complaints, decisions made without adequate consideration of relevant circumstances, failure to coordinate essential support, inability to obtain effective review, financial and practical disadvantage, and loss of confidence in complaint and accountability mechanisms.

My archive contains material relating to multiple Australian government and administrative systems.

The forensic question is whether the cumulative decisions and omissions, when reconstructed chronologically, demonstrate a pattern of institutional failure or disadvantage.

I use the term administrative annihilation to describe the cumulative experience I report.

It is a serious analytical characterisation, not a claim that a legally established coordinated operation has already been proved.

5. Social death and social erasure

Social death describes the experience of remaining physically alive while losing meaningful participation, recognition, belonging, relationships, support and standing within society.

Social erasure describes the process through which a person’s identity, testimony and history become marginalised, disregarded or displaced by descriptions supplied by others.

These concepts are particularly important when examining the circumstances of a vulnerable person who depends upon institutions, professional relationships and support systems to exercise ordinary rights and participate in society.

My testimony raises questions about the breakdown of relationships and support, the consequences of institutional disengagement, the effect of repeated credibility disputes, the loss of meaningful advocacy, the consequences of disability-related exclusion, the impact of being unable to obtain satisfactory answers, and the difficulty of communicating a complex history through fragmented complaint systems.

I describe this as social death because the consequences, as I experienced them, extended beyond paperwork.

They affected my ability to participate in ordinary human life.

A person can be socially erased without being physically absent.

The forensic examination must identify the actual events, decisions, communications and consequences that support this description.

6. The Legal Aid ban and exclusion from justice

One of the most serious dimensions of my account concerns access to legal representation.

I have reported being denied Legal Aid and experiencing substantial barriers to obtaining representation and pursuing matters through formal systems.

I describe the resulting experience as a Legal Aid ban and exclusion from justice.

That description requires particular forensic attention.

There is a difference between a formal prohibition on receiving legal assistance, a refusal of an individual application, a finding of ineligibility, a restriction on assistance for particular proceedings, repeated unsuccessful applications, and a practical inability to obtain representation.

The distinction matters because each has a different legal and evidentiary meaning.

The examination must identify the relevant Legal Aid decisions, application dates, refusal reasons, correspondence, review rights and any restrictions communicated.

But the underlying human question remains: how does a person obtain justice when they cannot secure the legal representation necessary to navigate the very systems through which they are seeking justice?

A right to seek review is not necessarily meaningful in practice if a person cannot access the assistance needed to exercise it.

I regard this as one of the central issues requiring independent examination.

7. Whistleblower retaliation

I have described myself as a whistleblower and have raised concerns about the treatment of my disclosures, the responses of relevant institutions and the consequences I believe followed.

The archive includes material concerning government correspondence, complaints, disclosures and institutional responses.

I allege that my attempts to raise concerns were not adequately addressed and that I experienced adverse treatment in connection with my efforts to speak out.

The forensic examination must establish what information was disclosed, to whom, when, whether the disclosure qualified for protection under the applicable whistleblower or public interest disclosure framework, what response or allocation decision followed, what adverse treatment subsequently occurred, whether there is documentary evidence connecting that treatment to the disclosure, and whether alternative explanations exist.

Whistleblower retaliation is a serious allegation.

It must be examined through chronology, documentary evidence, applicable statutory protections and the conduct of relevant decision-makers.

The absence of a formal finding does not make an allegation impossible to investigate.

Equally, the existence of a disclosure does not automatically prove retaliation.

The purpose of preservation is to make that examination possible.

Part III — Total human betrayal

8. When the systems of protection become the subject of the complaint

I use the expression total human betrayal to describe the cumulative personal experience of seeking protection, assistance, representation, understanding and accountability while feeling repeatedly abandoned by the people and systems from which those things should have been available.

This is not simply a complaint about one decision.

It concerns the cumulative human consequences of multiple failures or alleged failures across different areas of life: healthcare and hospital treatment, disability support and administration, legal representation and access to justice, whistleblower protection, government complaints and oversight, financial security, personal relationships and support, and institutional recognition and credibility.

The question is whether the individual events, when examined together, reveal a pattern of preventable harm, institutional fragmentation, inadequate accountability or failure to protect.

The phrase total human betrayal is my moral and personal description of that experience.

It does not require me to pretend that every individual involved had the same motive.

It does require that the cumulative consequences be taken seriously.

There is a difference between investigating individual decisions in isolation and examining the combined effect those decisions have had on one person’s life.

The whole human consequence must not disappear because responsibility is divided between departments.

Whistleblower retaliation — chronological documentary examination

Examination date: 9 November 2026. Status: preliminary archive-based forensic examination. Not an audited investigation, judicial finding or determination of liability.

There is documentary evidence of disclosures, institutional responses and procedural refusals or non-allocation. That is a legitimate basis for examining possible whistleblower retaliation. It does not, by itself, establish that every later adverse event was caused by a disclosure or that the agencies acted pursuant to a common plan.

The strongest documented starting point named in this examination is the Federal Court’s 27 March 2023 assessment. The wider archive also identifies earlier APRA/OAIC PID correspondence dated 22 October 2021, a 2023 NDIS PID response, Prime Ministerial and Attorney-General correspondence, and later oversight and service-restriction material. Events are kept in date order. Where the underlying original or full response still needs to be checked, that limit is stated.

Purpose

The examination follows one sequence: what was disclosed, to whom, when it was received, whether it was acknowledged, how it was classified, whether it was referred, whether it was investigated, declined, closed, not allocated or left unanswered, what adverse events followed, whether there is evidence connecting those events to the disclosures, and what remains unresolved. Chronology can establish sequence. It cannot, by itself, establish motive or causation.

It states that, on the information supplied, the author was or had been an employee with DSS providing services under the trading name Rich McLean, Arts Life Coach, Peer-Support Worker and Mental Health Advocate.

It states that the assessor found it difficult to determine whether the belief was based on reasonable grounds, but that, for the purposes of the initial assessment, he was prepared to assume that the conduct disclosed was disclosable conduct under the PID Act.

It then reaches a procedural conclusion. Tredwell was not satisfied that the disclosure related to the Federal Court, the Federal Circuit and Family Court, or the National Native Title Tribunal. He concluded that the correspondence had not been provided to an authorised recipient for an internal disclosure under the Act. He decided not to allocate the handling of the disclosure to any agency. He stated that no further action under the PID Act would be taken by the Federal Court or FCFCOA, or another Commonwealth agency, on that correspondence. He stated that he would not advise the Principal Officers of the Federal Court or FCFCOA of the disclosure or his decision. The letter identifies the Commonwealth Ombudsman as a possible complaint pathway.

Two elements must be kept apart. A substantive assessment which, for initial assessment, was prepared to assume that the conduct described was disclosable. A procedural decision not to allocate the material. The document does not establish that the alleged underlying wrongdoing occurred. It also does not establish that the decision not to allocate was retaliatory. It is a primary institutional record of what was disclosed, the statutory categories considered, and the reason given for the procedural outcome.
