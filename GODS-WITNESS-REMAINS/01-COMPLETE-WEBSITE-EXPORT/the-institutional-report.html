5. NDIS Code of Conduct

The relevant legislation is the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (Cth), particularly section 73V, together with section 6 of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (Code of Conduct) Rules 2018.

The Code requires covered persons to respect individual rights to freedom of expression, self-determination and decision-making; respect the privacy of people with disability; provide supports and services safely and competently, with care and skill; act with integrity, honesty and transparency; promptly raise and act on concerns about the quality and safety of supports; take reasonable steps to prevent and respond to violence, exploitation, neglect and abuse; and take reasonable steps to prevent and respond to sexual misconduct.

Section 73V makes compliance with the Code a statutory requirement. The Act provides civil penalties for contravention, including a higher penalty for serious contraventions by NDIS providers.

If the evidence establishes that Ablepoint or a covered worker failed to respect the participant’s decision-making, failed to respond to complaints, provided unsafe supports, disregarded privacy or failed to take reasonable steps to prevent or respond to abuse or neglect, the relevant Code obligations may be engaged. The investigation should not simply ask whether the provider has policies. It should determine whether those policies were followed in the actual circumstances.

The relevant records include service agreements, support plans, risk assessments, case notes, complaints and incident registers, staff communications, provider responses to requests to leave, internal investigations, and records of any referral to the NDIS Commission.

Official sources: NDIS Act. NDIS Code of Conduct.

Part III — Protected disclosures and victimisation

7. NDIS Act protected-disclosure provisions

Sections 73ZA–73ZD of the NDIS Act establish a specific framework for disclosures qualifying for protection. This is particularly important because Dr McLean identifies himself as an NDIS participant who has made complaints about an NDIS provider.

The statutory framework addresses disclosures qualifying for protection; protection from liability for qualifying disclosures; confidentiality of the discloser’s identity; prohibition of victimisation; and compensation where the statutory requirements are satisfied.

The relevant legal question is whether particular disclosures made by Dr McLean met the statutory requirements. For each disclosure, the investigation should identify the information disclosed; the date and method of disclosure; the recipient; whether the recipient was authorised under the Act; whether the disclosure concerned suspected contravention by an NDIS provider; whether the statutory reasonable-grounds requirement was met; whether the disclosure qualified for protection; whether any subsequent detriment or threat of detriment occurred; whether the alleged detriment was connected to the protected disclosure; and whether compensation or another remedy may be available.

The existence of a complaint does not automatically establish victimisation. The required statutory connection must be established. However, a qualifying disclosure must not be treated as an ordinary complaint stripped of its statutory protection.

Required determination: the NDIS Commission and NDIA should identify which disclosures qualified for protection and whether any conduct within their knowledge raises an issue under the victimisation provisions. Official source: NDIS Act.

8. Commonwealth Public Interest Disclosure Act

The Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth) provides a separate framework for disclosures concerning wrongdoing or maladministration within the Commonwealth public sector. The current compilation identified for this report is Compilation No. 20, effective 4 June 2026.

Relevant provisions include section 10, protection of disclosers; section 13, what constitutes taking a reprisal; sections 14–16, civil remedies; section 19, reprisal offences; section 20, protection of identifying information; section 29, disclosable conduct; sections 43–45, allocation of disclosures; sections 47–53, investigation arrangements; and sections 59–60, additional obligations of principal officers and authorised officers.

The Act’s objects include promoting integrity and accountability in the Commonwealth public sector and supporting and protecting eligible public officials who make qualifying disclosures. An important limitation is that the Commonwealth PID Act is not a general-purpose whistleblower statute for every member of the public. Eligibility and disclosure requirements must be satisfied.

Therefore, Dr McLean’s disclosures should be assessed individually to determine whether he was eligible to make a protected disclosure under the Act or whether another statutory protection applies. The investigation should not assume that rejection, delay or non-allocation of a disclosure automatically establishes reprisal. It should examine the statutory eligibility requirements, the decision-making record and any evidence of detriment connected with a protected disclosure.

Official source: Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013.

Part VI — Financial independence and NSW Trustee and Guardian

15. NSW Trustee and Guardian statutory principles

Section 39 of the NSW Trustee and Guardian Act 2009 (NSW) sets out general principles applicable to functions under the relevant Chapter. These principles include giving paramount consideration to the welfare and interests of the person; restricting freedom of decision and freedom of action as little as possible; encouraging the person to live a normal life in the community; taking the person’s views into consideration; recognising the importance of preserving family relationships and cultural and linguistic environments; encouraging self-reliance in personal, domestic and financial affairs; and protecting the person from neglect, abuse and exploitation.

These principles are relevant where an applicable financial management or guardianship arrangement affects the participant’s independence. The investigation must first identify the precise order and the statutory authority under which it was made. A financial management order and a guardianship order are not interchangeable. Their legal effects must not be assumed.

The investigation should obtain the original order; the court or tribunal that made it; the date and statutory basis; the reasons for the order; the scope of the appointed person’s authority; any instructions to financial institutions; any requests by Dr McLean for access to funds; the response to those requests; any review or variation applications; and any consideration of less restrictive arrangements.

Where access to money for food, medication, accommodation or other essential needs is affected, the institution should explain the legal basis and practical arrangements for meeting those needs. The relevant question is whether the restrictions were lawful, necessary, proportionate and consistent with the statutory principles. Official legislation: NSW Trustee and Guardian Act 2009.

Part VII — Legal Aid and access to justice

16. Legal Aid NSW

Dr McLean reports that Legal Aid NSW restricted or refused legal assistance in circumstances involving disability support, financial restrictions, provider complaints and police proceedings. He regards this as an inversion of the purpose of access to justice: the person seeking to challenge alleged wrongdoing may be unable to obtain the assistance needed to do so.

That concern warrants examination, but the legal analysis must distinguish between a refusal that is lawful under eligibility and merits criteria and a refusal that is affected by error, unfairness, discrimination or failure to consider relevant circumstances.

Important statutory correction: section 56 of the Legal Aid Commission Act 1979 (NSW) should not be cited as a general appeal provision for refusal of legal aid. The current section 56 concerns the Chief Executive Officer. The actual review or appeal rights must be identified from the relevant decision, the applicable provisions and Legal Aid NSW’s current procedures.

The investigation should obtain the original application; the decision notice; the date and identity of the decision-maker; the reasons for refusal or restriction; the policy and criteria applied; any relevant assessment of means and merits; any consideration of disability-related communication needs; any internal review or reconsideration request; and any advice about external review rights.

The practical effect of denying legal assistance may be serious where a person is seeking to challenge decisions affecting liberty, safety, money, accommodation or disability support. That practical effect should be recognised without alleging, in the absence of evidence, that Legal Aid intended to protect criminal conduct or a criminal enterprise. The investigation should determine whether the decision was properly made, whether the applicant was informed of available review options and whether disability-related adjustments were considered. Official legislation: Legal Aid Commission Act 1979.

Part VIII — Disability discrimination

17. Disability Discrimination Act 1992

The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth) is relevant to the provision of services and accommodation. Sections 5 and 6 address direct and indirect disability discrimination, including relevant failures to make reasonable adjustments. Section 24 concerns discrimination in the provision of goods, services and facilities. Section 25 concerns discrimination in accommodation.

The investigation should consider whether any organisation treated Dr McLean less favourably because of disability; imposed a condition that disadvantaged him because of disability; failed to make reasonable adjustments to communication or service access; failed to consider disability-related needs when responding to complaints; or restricted access to services or accommodation on discriminatory terms.

A person’s disability must not be used as a reason to disregard their testimony or deny them fair consideration. However, the statutory elements of discrimination must be established. A disagreement with an institutional decision is not, by itself, proof of unlawful discrimination. Official legislation: Disability Discrimination Act 1992.

Part IX — International human-rights obligations

18. Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities

The CRPD is particularly relevant to the reported circumstances. 19. International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

The ICCPR provisions potentially relevant include Article 2, the general obligation to respect and ensure Covenant rights without discrimination; Article 7, protection against torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment; Article 9, liberty and security of person; Article 14, equality before courts and tribunals and fair hearing guarantees; Article 17, protection against arbitrary or unlawful interference with privacy; and Article 26, equality before the law and equal protection.

20. International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

The ICESCR provisions potentially relevant include Article 6, the right to work; Article 9, the right to social security; Article 11, the right to an adequate standard of living, including adequate food and housing; and Article 12, the right to the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health. These provisions are relevant to the cumulative effects of financial insecurity, homelessness, inadequate support, reduced access to health care and loss of the practical means to live independently.

21. Convention against Torture

The CAT provisions requiring consideration include Article 2, the obligation to prevent torture; Article 12, prompt and impartial investigation where there is reasonable ground to believe torture has occurred; Article 13, the right to complain and have the complaint promptly and impartially examined; and Article 16, prevention of other acts of cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment within the Convention’s scope.

The legal definition of torture is specific. Article 1 requires the relevant elements, including the severity and intentional infliction of suffering, a specified purpose and the involvement or acquiescence of a public official or another person acting in an official capacity. The use of the word “torture” in a personal account does not automatically establish that legal definition. However, allegations of serious mistreatment, abuse, neglect or official acquiescence should be assessed under the relevant domestic and international standards.

22. Domestic legal effect of treaties

Australia’s ratification of a treaty creates international obligations. Ratification does not automatically make every treaty article a directly enforceable domestic cause of action. The domestic effect depends on implementing legislation and the relevant legal framework. The treaties nevertheless provide important standards for assessing government conduct, statutory interpretation, policy and institutional accountability.

Official international sources: CRPD; ICCPR; ICESCR; CAT; Australian Government international human-rights system.

Part XV — Conclusions and recommendations

36. Legal conclusions

On the material presently available, the following conclusions can responsibly be made.

First: the NDIS Act and Code impose statutory obligations on covered providers and workers concerning safety, competence, integrity, privacy, self-determination and responses to abuse, neglect and exploitation.

Second: the NDIS Act contains a specific protected-disclosure framework. The participant’s complaints should be assessed against sections 73ZA–73ZD, including the victimisation prohibition and compensation provisions where applicable.

Third: the Commonwealth PID Act provides a separate protection framework, but its requirements must be applied to each disclosure. It should not be assumed that every complaint made by a member of the public qualifies.

Fourth: the NSW Trustee and Guardian Act contains principles requiring attention to welfare, autonomy, the least possible restriction of freedom, self-reliance and protection from abuse and exploitation.

Fifth: Legal Aid NSW decisions must be assessed against the actual decision, applicable criteria and review mechanisms. Section 56 of the Legal Aid Commission Act should not be misrepresented as a general appeal right.

Sixth: disability discrimination law may be engaged where the statutory elements of direct or indirect discrimination, including failure to make reasonable adjustments, are established.

Seventh: Australia’s international treaty obligations provide important standards for assessing the treatment of people with disability, access to justice, personal safety, privacy, social security, housing and protection from prohibited mistreatment.
