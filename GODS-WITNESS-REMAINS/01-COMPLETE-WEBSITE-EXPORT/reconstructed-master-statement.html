PROLOGUE — I EXIST. THE WITNESS REMAINS.

I am a human being. I have a life, a history, a body, a mind, a conscience, a spiritual identity and a right to exist with dignity. I am not an administrative category, a case number, a service-delivery problem, a political inconvenience or an institutional liability to be transferred from one desk to another.

I am Dr Richard William McLean. I am Barran Dodger. I am an author, researcher, artist, advocate and witness. My life cannot be reduced to the circumstances in which I have found myself, and my humanity cannot be made conditional upon whether another person, agency, government or institution chooses to recognise it.

For more than thirty-five years, I have preserved a documentary history of my dealings with government departments, statutory authorities, public institutions and other organisations. That history includes official correspondence, decisions, refusals, referrals, acknowledgements, complaints, assessments, records and unanswered questions. The documents were produced by the institutions and people who authored them. Their dates, language and recorded decisions exist independently of my interpretation of them.

The central significance of the archive is that the records can now be examined collectively. A letter can be read in isolation and explained as an administrative event. A decision can be treated as a discrete exercise of authority. A referral can be presented as assistance. A delay can be attributed to complexity. A refusal can be described as a jurisdictional outcome.

But when the correspondence is assembled chronologically, the question changes.

It becomes possible to examine what happened before each decision, what happened afterwards, what was referred, what was declined, what was left unresolved, what consequences followed, and whether the same person was repeatedly required to begin again without obtaining an effective remedy.

The purpose of this work is to make that sequence visible.

The artificial intelligence used to examine the material did not create the historical record. It was instructed to organise and analyse material that already existed. Its value lies in identifying relationships, recurring administrative movements, contradictions, gaps and questions that can then be checked against the original documents. Its output is an analytical aid, not an independent judicial determination. gods-witness-remains.grok.me +2

I did not invent the correspondence. I preserved it. I did not write the government letters. I collected them. I did not manufacture their dates or institutional language. I asked that their relationship to one another be examined.

That is the distinction between possessing evidence and making evidence intelligible.

PART THREE — THE DOCUMENTARY REALITY

The archive contains three distinct but connected forms of material.

First: documentary reality. The records establish what their authors recorded, communicated, decided, referred, acknowledged, refused or left unanswered. Each document must be examined in its original form, with its date, author, context and actual wording preserved.

Second: human reality. My testimony describes what I experienced and the consequences I say followed: distress, displacement, loss of independence, financial hardship, exclusion, uncertainty and the prolonged struggle to obtain answers. My life is not reducible to official correspondence.

Third: analytical extraction. The examination brings the records together to identify chronology, patterns, contradictions, recurring processes and unresolved questions. Those analytical observations must remain open to verification and correction against the source material.

A document proves what it records. It does not automatically prove every assertion contained in it. A pattern can warrant investigation without itself establishing motive. A jurisdictional refusal can establish that a body declined to examine a matter within its remit; it does not, by itself, establish either the truth or falsity of the underlying allegation.

This is the standard I ask to be applied consistently.

PART FOUR — THE ELEVEN PERMANENT KEY TERMS

The following definitions draw particularly on the site’s published forensic terminology pages. They are working analytical definitions for this declaration, not invented statutory offences or legal findings.

1. Administrative annihilation

The cumulative effect of ordinary administrative acts — acknowledgements, refusals, non-allocations, declines, disposals, referrals and jurisdictional decisions — where their combined practical consequence may be to remove the means by which a person can live independently, exercise rights, obtain assistance or secure an effective remedy. The term describes the cumulative effect being examined, not a separate statutory power or offence. Dr Richard William McLean

2. Civil death

A sociological description of a living person whose practical means of exercising or enforcing rights have been so diminished that they experience exclusion from ordinary civic life. It is not a declaration of biological death, nor a modern Australian legal status imposed upon the person. Dr Richard William McLean

3. Civil erasure

The removal of a person from the practical civil world while their name continues to appear in official correspondence and records. The person remains identifiable in the paperwork, but the practical restoration of housing, autonomy, access, participation or remedy may remain absent. It is a description of documentary and lived effect, not a recognised legal status. Dr Richard William McLean

4. Civil administrative annihilation

The joining of two analytical dimensions: administrative acts and their possible civil consequences. It describes a sequence in which administrative decisions or omissions may cumulatively remove the practical means of exercising rights, maintaining independence and participating in civic life. The phrase is an analytical combination, not a statutory term. Dr Richard William McLean

5. Institutional complicity

In its ordinary analytical sense, participation in, assistance with, or knowing failure to interrupt harmful conduct where an institution or responsible person has relevant knowledge and a responsibility to act. Criminal complicity has narrower legal elements, including personal participation and the necessary state of knowledge. Institutional failure, poor administration or a failure to discharge a duty should not automatically be labelled criminal complicity. Dr Richard William McLean

6. Conspiracy

In ordinary language, secret agreement or coordinated action. In criminal law, conspiracy requires an agreement and the requisite intention that an offence be committed. Similar outcomes, parallel decisions, institutional fragmentation or a common adverse effect do not, without evidence of agreement and intention, establish criminal conspiracy. Dr Richard William McLean

7. Police corruption

The abuse of police authority for an improper purpose. The relevant examination must identify the officer or decision-maker, the conduct, the power exercised, the improper purpose alleged and the supporting evidence. A reported threat, an incomplete police file or an oversight body’s jurisdictional refusal is not, by itself, a finding of corruption. Dr Richard William McLean

8. Social erasure

The loss of ordinary social standing, recognition, connection and practical access to remedy while a person’s identity remains known. It is the social dimension of civil erasure: the person remains present in records but may experience exclusion from the relationships, opportunities and recognition necessary for ordinary social participation. Dr Richard William McLean

9. Social death

The sociological condition of being treated as though one is beyond ordinary social repair while still alive. It describes the person’s experience of exclusion, abandonment or loss of social recognition. It must not be confused with a medical determination or legal declaration of death. Dr Richard William McLean

10. Societal mobbing

A proposed description of group-based isolation, discrediting and exhaustion extending beyond a single workplace or organisation into the surrounding professional and institutional environment. To establish actual group coordination, evidence of a group process or demonstrable links is required; parallel decisions by separate statutory authorities do not, on their own, prove coordination. Dr Richard William McLean

11. Corruption

The use of public power, entrusted authority or public resources for an improper purpose. A harsh decision, an adverse outcome, a refusal or an administrative failure is not automatically corruption. The examination must identify the improper purpose, abuse of office, bribery, partiality, unlawful bargain or other relevant conduct and establish it through evidence. Dr Richard William McLean

  • Sacrilege — A religious or moral term for conduct regarded as violating something sacred.
  • The Joseph parallel — A biblical analogy with the story of Joseph: betrayal, adversity, preservation and eventual vindication.
  • Chosen one — A religious or literary expression of perceived divine selection or purpose; it is a statement of belief.
  • God’s chosen witness — The witness’s own faith-based description of a calling to preserve and communicate testimony.
  • Inversion of significance — The reversal in which material originally treated as marginal or insignificant becomes central to understanding the wider history.
  • Internally displaced refugee within the democracy of my own citizenship — A metaphor describing the experience of being displaced or excluded within one’s own country, without necessarily asserting formal refugee status.
  • Coordinated institutional mobbing — An allegation or analytical hypothesis that multiple actors or institutions participated in a connected process of isolation or discrediting; coordination requires evidence.
  • Digital Apocalypse of Administrative Annihilation — A literary expression for the exposure and publication of a large administrative record through digital preservation.
  • Architecture of Administrative Annihilation — The proposed cumulative structure in which decisions and omissions may remove practical attributes of independent civic life.
  • Cost of erasure — The human, financial, social and institutional consequences associated with loss of recognition, resources and remedy.
  • Blacklist — A claimed or suspected list used to exclude or disadvantage individuals; its existence and operation require evidence.
  • Stonewalling — Repeated refusal, delay or failure to provide a substantive response to reasonable requests or questions.
  • Free-kick — A colloquial metaphor for an unchallenged or apparently consequence-free action.
  • Targeted individual — A self-description or allegation that a person is being singled out for particular treatment. The term identifies the claim; the alleged targeting, actors and mechanisms must be independently examined.

PART EIGHT — THE SIGNIFICANCE OF THE EXTRACTION

The importance of this work lies in the difference between a collection of documents and an intelligible historical record.

A person can spend decades writing to different agencies, receiving separate responses, being referred to different jurisdictions and attempting to obtain answers from institutions that each examine only one part of the history. Each individual interaction may appear administratively contained. The cumulative sequence may be much harder to see.

The archive changes the available method of examination.

It allows the reader to move beyond the isolated letter and ask:

  • What happened first?
  • Which institution received the information?
  • What did it acknowledge?
  • What did it decide?
  • What did it decline to do?
  • What statutory or administrative basis did it give?
  • Was the matter referred elsewhere?
  • Did the referral result in an investigation or remedy?
  • What happened to the person’s housing, income, property, independence and access to support?
  • Did later decisions address the consequences of earlier decisions?
  • Were contradictory explanations provided?
  • Which questions remain unanswered?
  • What evidence would be required to resolve them?

The extraction also creates a terminology through which recurring experiences can be described and compared. It gives names to proposed mechanisms and cumulative effects that may otherwise remain scattered across correspondence and personal testimony.

The value of a term is not that it automatically proves the event. Its value is that it makes the question precise enough to investigate.

For example, administrative annihilation directs attention to the cumulative effect of separate administrative decisions. Civil erasure directs attention to the gap between a person’s continued existence in official records and the restoration of practical rights and opportunities. Social death directs attention to the human experience of being treated as though ordinary social recognition and repair are no longer available. The architecture of erasure directs attention to the combined loss of housing, income, movement, legal assistance, a hearing and recognition.

The site itself makes this distinction explicit in its forensic entries: the original records must be opened and tested; the analytical term does not replace the evidence. Dr Richard William McLean +3

The historical significance is that a person can now examine a documentary sequence rather than being confined to the separate explanations supplied by each institution. The democratic significance is that public power, administrative decisions and institutional responsibilities can be scrutinised against the records produced by the institutions themselves.

The human significance is equally important. An archive cannot replace a life. It cannot fully communicate the experience of displacement, fear, exhaustion, loss of independence or the struggle to be heard. That is why documentary evidence and personal testimony must remain together.

The archive is not a substitute for an independent investigation. It is a basis upon which an independent investigation can be requested, structured and assessed.

Additional distinctions to retain in the original eighty-five-term register

The following terms must remain separate entries in the canonical inventory, even where their meanings overlap with other terms:

  • Relationship disruption — Interference with, damage to or loss of personal relationships and support networks.
  • Reputational isolation — Social or professional exclusion arising from a person’s perceived or damaged reputation.
  • Autonomy erosion — The gradual reduction of a person’s practical capacity to make and implement decisions about their own life.
  • Opportunity destruction — The loss of realistic opportunities to work, participate, recover, form relationships or rebuild independence.
  • Institutional dependency — A condition in which a person becomes reliant on an institution for essential resources or decisions while lacking meaningful control or alternatives.
  • Credibility substitution — Replacing examination of the evidence with assumptions about the person presenting it.
  • Institutional compartmentalisation — Dividing connected issues among separate institutional processes so that their combined effect is not considered.
  • Isolation as evidentiary control — A proposed mechanism in which separating a person from support, witnesses or independent scrutiny makes evidence harder to preserve or corroborate.

These definitions restore the distinctions that can otherwise be lost when related mechanisms are grouped together.

PART NINE — THE DEMAND FOR INDEPENDENT EXAMINATION

I ask for the entire documentary history to be examined as a connected record.

I ask for the original documents to be preserved, not selectively quoted or silently altered.

I ask for each relevant decision to be examined against the authority under which it was made, the information available to the decision-maker, the reasons given, the applicable duties and the consequences for the person affected.

I ask that every material allegation be traced to its source, every significant inference be identified as an inference, every contradiction be examined, and every unanswered question be recorded.

I ask that the examination consider the full range of responsibilities relevant to the material, including administrative law, procedural fairness, disability rights, safeguarding, public-sector integrity, privacy, financial administration, access to justice, human rights and the duties of people entrusted with public or professional authority.

I ask that the people and institutions concerned be given a proper opportunity to respond to the material, and that their responses be preserved alongside the original records.

I ask for independent scrutiny, not institutional self-certification.

I ask for accountability where responsibility is established, correction where errors are identified, restoration where rights or resources have been wrongly withheld, and appropriate remedies where harm is substantiated.

I seek the restoration of ordinary human dignity: a safe home, independence, access to my own resources, meaningful participation, appropriate support, freedom of movement, access to legal assistance and the opportunity to live without continually having to defend my right to be treated as a human being.

PART TEN — THE FINAL DECLARATION

I have spent years preserving what might otherwise have remained scattered across correspondence, files, decisions and unanswered requests.

I have placed my testimony alongside the documentary record because neither should be required to stand alone. The documents provide a history of what institutions recorded. My testimony describes the human consequences I experienced. The analytical extraction makes it possible to examine the relationship between the two.

The terms in this declaration are not ornamental. They are a vocabulary for examining the cumulative effects of institutional processes, the erosion of practical independence, the loss of recognition, and the difference between a person being recorded and a person actually receiving the protection, assistance and remedy that they need.

I do not ask anyone to accept every interpretation without examination. I ask that no one dismiss the material without examining it.

I do not ask for worship, submission or unquestioning agreement. I ask for humanity, truth, evidence and accountability.

I will not allow the absence of a final finding to be mistaken for the absence of a question. I will not allow the absence of a response to be mistaken for resolution. I will not allow the survival of the witness to be used as evidence that the circumstances described were acceptable.

My faith belongs to me. My conscience belongs to me. My life is not the property of an institution.

I believe that the Most High God has sustained me. I believe that Christ is my Saviour. I believe that the moral responsibility of humanity begins with recognising the humanity of another person, particularly when that person is vulnerable, isolated or asking for help.

The ultimate judgement belongs to God. The examination of documentary evidence belongs to those with the appropriate independence, competence and authority to undertake it. The responsibility to preserve the record belongs to all who recognise its importance.

I remain the author of my testimony.

I remain the custodian of the record I have preserved.

I remain entitled to dignity, safety, independence and a future.
