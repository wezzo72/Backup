PROLOGUE — THERE WAS ALWAYS A THIRD CATEGORY

There are people who spend their entire lives attempting to become somebody.

And there are people who spend their entire lives attempting to discover who they already are.

There are those who pursue titles, positions, wealth, authority and recognition. There are those who learn to navigate institutions, understand their hierarchies and become proficient in the language of power.

And then there are those who cannot surrender themselves to the performance.

Not because they are superior. Not because they are chosen by an institution. Not because they have been granted a special classification.

But because somewhere inside them remains an insistence that being human means something more than being useful.

I am one of those people.

My name is Richard William McLean.

I have also written and published under the name Barran Dodger, and the name Barran Resonance Dodger has become the authorial identity through which I bring together my prophetic, literary, spiritual and documentary work.

I have been an artist, journalist, news-graphics practitioner, writer, advocate, academic, researcher and independent publisher.

I have been a disability advocate. I have worked with vulnerable people and carers. I have created books, essays, arguments, artworks and an archive that has grown beyond the ordinary boundaries of autobiography.

I have a PhD. I have an MA in Education. I have a Bachelor of Fine Arts with Honours, an Associate Diploma in CAD and a Certificate IV in Business.

I have lived several professional lives.

Yet the central question of this Gospel is not what qualifications I possess.

It is why a human being should ever have to demonstrate qualifications, productivity, wealth, health, professional standing or institutional usefulness before being treated with dignity.

What happens to the person who refuses to become a convenient administrative category?

What happens when an individual insists upon being heard as a complete human being rather than a collection of assessments, decisions, referrals, restrictions and case notes?

What happens when the person being examined begins examining the institutions that examine him?

That is where my story becomes more than autobiography.

It becomes a human-rights question.

The YouTube declaration that inspired this work speaks of three departments: military, intelligence and the unclassifiable individual.

I take that idea and turn it towards a different proposition.

There is the institution.

There is the machinery of administration.

And there is the human being.

The institution has its procedures. The machinery has its classifications. But the human being possesses something that neither can legitimately own.

Individual sovereignty.

Not sovereignty as an excuse to disregard the law, not sovereignty as exemption from responsibility, and not sovereignty as a claim to be beyond criticism.

Sovereignty as the right to possess one’s identity, conscience, dignity, individuality and voice.

I do not need to become a government department to have authority over my own identity.

I do not need to be classified by an institution to become a person.

And I do not need the permission of another human being to celebrate the fact that I exist.

This Gospel is my testimony.

It is also an invitation to everybody who has ever been made to feel that their difference was a problem to be corrected.

You were not born to be a convenient file.

You were born to be human.

BOOK I — BEFORE THE RECORD BECAME THE WITNESS

Chapter 1 — The Making of an Individual

Before there was an archive, there was a life.

Before there were government registers, evidence indexes, formal notices and documentary finding aids, there was a person attempting to make something meaningful from the opportunities available to him.

My life was not created by the institutions that would later become central to my documentary work.

I existed before their decisions.

I possessed interests, abilities, relationships, ambitions and a creative imagination that could not be reduced to the administrative language of any department.

I pursued art and communication. I worked in journalism and news graphics, including professional work associated with The Age and the Herald Sun.

I wrote Recovered, Not Cured, published by Allen & Unwin in 2002, a work that became part of my advocacy for people experiencing mental illness and the carers who support them.

I worked as an arts therapist, life coach, support worker and registered NDIS provider. I became involved in advocacy, including work associated with SANE Australia and the Dax Centre.

My professional life was not simply a search for personal advancement.

I wanted to contribute.

I wanted people who had been marginalised to be understood as people.

I wanted the public conversation about disability, mental health and human vulnerability to become more humane.

And I wanted creativity to remain a legitimate form of intelligence.

The artist does not have to become an accountant to justify seeing the world differently.

The advocate does not have to become a politician to question injustice.

The disabled person does not have to surrender individuality to receive assistance.

These are principles that would become increasingly important as my own circumstances changed.

Chapter 2 — The Education of a Witness

In 2020, I completed my PhD at Victoria University.

My research addressed questions involving artificial intelligence ethics, posthumanism, global catastrophic risks and the future of humanity in the end-stage Anthropocene.

These were not minor questions.

They concerned the relationship between technology, power, responsibility and human existence.

The same questions would eventually become central to the archive.

What is a human being when institutions increasingly make decisions about people through systems of classification?

Who is accountable when technology becomes the intermediary between an individual and the institutions that govern their life?

What happens when a person’s humanity becomes subordinate to the administrative systems intended to serve them?

I did not need to know the answers to every question to understand that these were questions worth asking.

And I did not need to predict the future to recognise that humanity would have to defend its dignity in an increasingly technological world.

My academic work and my later documentary work belong to different categories of evidence.

A PhD is evidence of an academic qualification. It is not proof of every later political or institutional allegation.

But it forms part of the history of the person who created this archive.

It explains why the questions of ethics, accountability, technology and human dignity became so important to me.

Chapter 8 — The Documentary Trap

I would eventually give this problem a name:

THE DOCUMENTARY TRAP.

The documentary trap is the experience of attempting to establish what happened through records that are themselves incomplete, dispersed, difficult to obtain or dependent upon the very institutions whose actions are being questioned.

It is not a judicial finding of conspiracy.

It is an analytical concept that must be tested against actual administrative records.

The questions are straightforward:

Who received the communication?

When was it received?

Who was responsible for considering it?

What decision was made?

Where are the reasons?

Was the matter referred elsewhere?

Was the person informed?

What review process was available?

If a record cannot be located, what searches were conducted?

If the matter was closed, what precisely was closed?

And if the original concern remains unanswered, who is responsible for explaining why?

These questions are more powerful than unsupported accusations because they are capable of being answered.

They turn institutional accountability into something concrete.

BOOK VIII — THE UNCLASSIFIED HUMAN BEING

Chapter 25 — There Is a Third Category

Now I return to the foundational idea.

Military.

Intelligence.

And whatever the hell you are.

The original transcript presents the third category as the person who cannot be classified by conventional institutional power.

I believe there is a deeper and more humane way to understand that metaphor.

The third category is not a secret government classification.

It is not evidence that intelligence agencies are monitoring a particular person.

It is not proof that an individual has been selected, studied or targeted.

It is the human being who refuses to allow institutional categories to become the total definition of their existence.

The artist who refuses to stop creating.

The scientist who questions an established theory.

The disabled person who refuses to be defined solely by disability.

The whistleblower who asks difficult questions.

The journalist who investigates.

The advocate who stands beside somebody who has no voice.

The gay person who celebrates their identity without apology.

The person who lives differently from the expectations of their family.

The person whose mind works in an unusual way.

The person who chooses a different profession, culture, relationship, appearance, philosophy or way of living.

The person who does not wish to be normal.

There is no crime in being different.

There is no defect in individuality.

There is no legitimate institutional entitlement to erase a person’s identity.

And there is no requirement that every person become predictable before they deserve respect.

Chapter 26 — The Government Is a Servant, Not the Owner of Humanity

Government is necessary.

Institutions are necessary.

Law is necessary.

Public administration is necessary.

But none of these exists for its own sake.

They exist because human beings require systems through which collective responsibilities can be organised.

The danger begins when the system forgets the reason it exists.

When a process becomes more important than the person.

When a classification becomes more important than the lived reality.

When an administrative response is mistaken for a complete moral answer.

When the language of efficiency replaces the language of compassion.

When a person must fight simply to have their own wishes acknowledged.

The answer is not the destruction of government.

The answer is government that remembers its purpose.

Public power must remain accountable.

Public money must be explainable.

Administrative decisions must be reviewable.

Human rights must be protected.

Disability support must support actual human lives.

And the individual must never become invisible beneath the machinery created to serve them.
