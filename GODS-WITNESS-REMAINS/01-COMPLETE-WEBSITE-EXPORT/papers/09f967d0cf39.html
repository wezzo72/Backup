There is a profound difference between investigating a person and investigating a claim about that person. An investigation begins with a question. It identifies what is known, what remains uncertain, which records exist, who created them, what they actually say, and whether the available information supports the proposition being examined. A prejudgment begins somewhere else. It begins with a conclusion and searches for material that might sustain it. The difference is not merely philosophical. It determines what information gets collected, which documents receive attention, whether contradictory material is preserved, whether explanations are considered, and whether an administrative record becomes an instrument for discovering what happened or a mechanism for perpetuating an earlier assumption. PART I — THE DESK AT WHICH A CONCLUSION BECOMES A STORY Imagine a desk covered with documents. There are letters, emails, referral numbers, administrative decisions, responses to complaints, records of requests, and explanations supplied by the person whose life has become the subject of scrutiny. Every document has a date. Every correspondence has a sender and a recipient. Some records contain explicit decisions. Others merely acknowledge receipt, refer a matter elsewhere, impose a limitation, or explain why a particular process cannot proceed. The temptation is to treat the existence of a file as proof of the entire story surrounding it. That temptation must be resisted. The existence of a complaint does not establish the truth of every allegation in it. A refusal does not establish that the underlying complaint was false. A referral does not establish that misconduct occurred. If a person alleges serious detriment and produces documentary correspondence, it is equally wrong to dismiss the matter merely because no single letter contains the complete answer. An institution’s documented position is evidence of what the institution said. ● ● ● ● This is a basic question of evidence provenance. Who first made the assertion? Did subsequent speakers have direct knowledge? Did they see the original record? Did they obtain independent information? Were they relying on the same summary, the same witness, or the same interpretation? The distinction is essential when assessing documentary allegations. A responsible evidence register should distinguish at least four categories: Direct documentary support: a source explicitly records the fact being asserted. Corroboration: another independent source supports the same proposition. Inference: the proposition is a reasoned interpretation of the available records rather than an explicit statement in them. A letter that records that a complaint was made directly supports the proposition that the complaint was made. It does not necessarily prove the conduct alleged in the complaint.
