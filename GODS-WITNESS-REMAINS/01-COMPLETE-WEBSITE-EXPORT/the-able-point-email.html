The declaration

I refused to die in the ashes of human betrayal.

But I survived.

The darkness and the rebuilding

I looked directly into my own darkness. And I am not merely the person who survived. I am the person who continued to dream, who found the strength to heal, who rebuilt without the rescue that never came, and who still believes that a better life is possible.

I have known the darkness of betrayal, but I will not make betrayal my identity. I have experienced the consequences of abandonment, but I will not abandon myself. FORMAL CRIMINAL COMPLAINT AND FORENSIC INVESTIGATION DOSSIER. THE ABLE POINT ACCOUNTABILITY DOSSIER. Alleged financial misconduct, NDIS fraud, neglect, safeguarding failures, financial exploitation, administrative misconduct, human rights violations.

Prepared: 30 October 2026. Complainant: Dr Richard William McLean, publicly known as Dr Barran Dodger. Organisation complained of: Able Point Australia. Jurisdiction: New South Wales and the Commonwealth of Australia. Subject: investigation of organisational and individual responsibility for alleged misconduct in the provision, administration and safeguarding of disability supports and accommodation.

Part I — Purpose and nature of the complaint

1. Formal criminal accusation

I formally request a comprehensive criminal, financial, regulatory, safeguarding and human-rights investigation into the conduct of Able Point Australia, including the conduct of its directors, executives, management, employees, contractors and any other persons who participated in decisions affecting my accommodation, disability supports, finances, personal safety and fundamental rights.

I allege that my experience raises serious questions about whether the organisation fulfilled its statutory obligations, whether funding was properly claimed and accounted for, whether support services were actually delivered, whether foreseeable risks were adequately addressed, and whether decisions affecting my independence and welfare were made lawfully.

I request investigation of possible criminal offences, including fraud, obtaining financial advantage by deception, false or misleading records, and any other offences supported by the evidence. I also request investigation of possible NDIS regulatory breaches, failures of professional ethics, disability discrimination, financial abuse, neglect, breaches of privacy and failures to respect internationally recognised human rights.

This complaint is not limited to an isolated missed appointment or disagreement about service quality. It concerns the cumulative effect of accommodation arrangements, support delivery, financial administration, safeguarding decisions and the treatment of a person dependent on disability services.

The central investigative question is whether Able Point exercised its responsibilities lawfully, honestly, competently and in the interests of the person receiving its services — and whether any individual or organisational misconduct caused financial, physical, psychological or other harm.

2. Evidentiary standard

This dossier distinguishes: documented fact, supported by an identifiable record; first-person testimony, an account provided by the complainant; allegation, conduct requiring investigation; inference, a possible explanation requiring corroboration; legal issue, a statutory or regulatory provision potentially engaged; finding, a conclusion that should only be made after proper investigation.

No individual is declared criminally guilty by this complaint. The purpose is to identify suspected conduct, preserve evidence, establish responsibility and request investigation by authorities with appropriate powers.

Part II — Particulars of the alleged conduct

3. AP-01. Failure to provide support and alleged abandonment

Allegation AP-01: failure to respond adequately to reported needs and requests for assistance. My account is that I repeatedly communicated concerns about abuse, neglect and personal safety; poverty and lack of access to essential resources; difficulties leaving accommodation where I did not feel safe; threats and the need for practical protection; difficulties obtaining transport and accessing necessary appointments; the need for assistance concerning my dog, Crystal; and the absence of an adequate management response to my complaints.

I have specifically questioned what happened after staff were informed that I could not leave, that I felt unsafe, and that I required practical assistance. The relevant documentary account appears in The Final Reckoning — The Barran Dodger Master Humanitarian and Documentary Judgment, pages 216–217.

Investigation required: identify every request for assistance received by Able Point; the date, recipient and contents of each communication; the person responsible for responding; the internal response and escalation records; whether assistance was offered, refused, delayed or never arranged; whether management knew that the situation was deteriorating; and the consequences of any failure to respond.

Provisions named for an investigator, not as a finding already made: NDIS Act 2013, section 73V, the Code of Conduct; sections 73Y–73Z, incident management and reportable incidents; the NDIS Practice Standards; and common-law negligence only if duty, breach, causation and damage are established. The four datasets the dossier says must be compared are NDIS funding claims, provider attendance, service documentation, and the participant’s own messages and complaints.

4. AP-02. Failure to respond to threats and safeguarding concerns

I have described a serious threat to kill, police involvement and a matter before Wyong Court. I have also alleged that Able Point declined to make a report or statement about circumstances I brought to its attention. These matters require examination independently of whether a criminal prosecution of the person who made the threat proceeds. The relevant account appears in the documentary record, pages 29–30 and 216–217.

The investigation must establish exactly what threat was communicated, which employee or manager received it, whether it was acknowledged, whether a risk assessment was completed, whether an incident report was created, whether police or emergency services were contacted where appropriate, whether a safety or relocation plan was considered, and whether the provider documented reasons for any decision not to take action.

The NDIS Code requires providers and workers to promptly raise and act on concerns affecting support quality and safety. Registered providers must notify the Commission of abuse or neglect within 24 hours of becoming aware of a reportable incident. The dossier states the limit itself: this is not a general obligation to report every threat made by an unrelated third party. The connection to the provision of supports, and the statutory incident category, must be established. Reportable incidents are set out by the NDIS Commission.

5. AP-03. Potential NDIS funding fraud and false claims

Priority criminal investigation. Allegation AP-03: possible financial advantage obtained for support services that were not delivered. I allege that support payments continued despite failures of attendance and service delivery. This allegation must be tested against the actual NDIA payment records. The fact that a provider continued receiving money does not establish fraud unless the payment was improperly claimed or obtained and the required knowledge and dishonesty are established.

The forensic examination must compare four independent datasets: NDIS funding (claims, payment dates, line items, quantities and amounts); provider attendance (worker rosters, clock-in records and actual attendance); service documentation (progress notes, daily logs and participant records); and participant evidence (messages, complaints, missed appointments and contemporaneous observations). Each disputed transaction should be examined individually. The potential overclaim is the sum of amounts claimed for unsupported services.

Legislation named: NDIS Act section 59AC, obtaining a payment that is not payable; section 59AD, obtaining a payment through fraud; section 59AE, concealing or falsifying required records; section 59A, knowingly providing false or misleading information. The dossier records that the serious-contravention maximum for section 59AD is 10 years’ imprisonment or 2,000 penalty units, or both, and that the ordinary offence carries a different penalty. NSW Crimes Act 1900: section 192E, fraud by deception; section 192F, dishonestly destroying or concealing accounting records; section 192G, dishonestly making or publishing a false statement. Section 192E carries a maximum of 10 years. The Acts are the NDIS Act and the Crimes Act 1900 (NSW). I request that the NDIA and the NDIS Commission independently audit the claims associated with my supports.

6. AP-04. Financial exploitation and accommodation

I have described an arrangement involving approximately $200 per week in rent, a lack of a signed accommodation agreement, and financial arrangements administered through NSW Trustee & Guardian. The investigation must establish who legally owns or operates the accommodation; whether Able Point is the accommodation provider, the support provider, or both; whether rent was collected by Able Point or another entity; whether the amounts were authorised and correctly accounted for; whether accommodation and support payments were separated; whether I was given clear information about charges; and whether any money was withheld or transferred without lawful authority.

The absence of a signed agreement is not automatically criminal. It makes the underlying financial arrangements and authority particularly important. Frameworks named: the NDIS Act and pricing rules; the Australian Consumer Law where it applies; NSW residential tenancy law, depending on what the accommodation legally was; Crimes Act section 192E if dishonest deception and a financial advantage are established; and the NSW Trustee & Guardian legislation where that agency’s decisions are involved. I request a full reconciliation of accommodation payments and disability support payments, without assuming the two are interchangeable.

7. AP-05. Complaints and incident management

The issue is not merely whether Able Point had a complaints policy. The question is whether that policy was implemented when I raised serious concerns. Under section 73W of the NDIS Act, registered providers must maintain a complaints system, including arrangements for independent advocates. Section 73Y requires an incident management system. Section 73Z addresses reportable incidents.

I request examination of complaint receipt dates, acknowledgements, internal investigations, findings and reasons, management escalation, advocacy arrangements, incident classifications, notifications to the NDIS Commission, corrective action, and whether complaints were closed without resolution.

8. AP-06. Possible retaliation and interference with autonomy

I have described repeated attempts to obtain answers, identify responsible management, challenge decisions and seek assistance to leave circumstances in which I did not feel safe. I allege that my requests did not produce the practical accountability and assistance I required. The investigation should establish whether any adverse treatment occurred because I exercised a right to complain, seek advocacy, express concerns or request a change of provider.

It should examine whether support was withdrawn or reduced after complaints, whether access to management was restricted, whether staff were instructed not to communicate with me, whether complaints were inaccurately characterised, whether decisions were made without consulting me, whether I was supported to change providers, and whether any person attempted to discourage or obstruct complaints. The legal question is whether particular conduct contravened the NDIS Code, a contract, anti-discrimination legislation or another law. The dossier states that retaliation is not automatically a separate criminal offence in every provider dispute.

9. AP-07. Possible disability discrimination

I request investigation into whether I was treated less favourably, denied reasonable access to services, or subjected to discriminatory conditions because of actual or perceived disability: whether communication was accessible, whether requests were considered on their merits, whether I was afforded meaningful choice and control, whether disability-related needs were accommodated, whether I was discouraged from exercising independence, and whether assumptions about disability affected the credibility given to my complaints.

Legislation named: Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth), sections 5, 6, 8 and 24, on the Federal Register; and the Anti-Discrimination Act 1977 (NSW), on NSW Legislation. A discrimination finding requires the statutory tests. Poor service to a person with disability is not, by itself, that finding.

10. AP-08. Human rights and dignity

I request an investigation into whether the cumulative treatment I describe was inconsistent with the protections governing disability support. The principles named are: CRPD article 12, equal recognition before the law, and whether autonomy and legal capacity were respected; article 16, freedom from exploitation, violence and abuse, and whether foreseeable risks were recognised; article 19, independent living, and whether I was supported to choose where and with whom I lived; article 22, privacy; article 28, an adequate standard of living; article 13, access to justice; and NDIS Act section 4, dignity, choice, participation, freedom from abuse and neglect, and the ability to pursue a grievance. The Convention is the CRPD. It binds Australia as a treaty. It does not automatically create a separate criminal offence every time a provider falls short.

11. Ethical and professional obligations

The NDIS Code of Conduct, as named in the email, requires providers and workers to respect individual rights and self-determination, respect privacy, provide safe and competent supports, act with integrity, honesty and transparency, and promptly raise and act on concerns about safety and service quality. The Code is published by the Commission.

Where Able Point was registered, the email asks for compliance with the Practice Standards on person-centred supports, individual values, privacy and dignity, independence and informed choice, violence, abuse, neglect, exploitation and discrimination, governance, risk, complaints, incidents, and the provision of supports. The Standards require allegations of abuse, neglect and exploitation to be acted on, recorded and investigated. They are the Practice Standards.

On governance, the email asks whether management supervised workers, responded to complaints independently, protected confidential information, kept accurate records, avoided conflicts of interest, ensured workers understood safeguarding, corrected service failures, and gave transparent explanations. These are proposed audit criteria, not a claim that every item is an independent criminal offence.
