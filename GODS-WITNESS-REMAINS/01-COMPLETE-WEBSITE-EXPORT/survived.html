PART III — THE SYSTEM’S OWN ETHICS TURNED BACK ON ITSELF

10. Standards, obligations and the questions each institution must answer

I am not asking these institutions to apply a standard invented for my benefit. I am requiring them to apply the standards they already claim to uphold: procedural fairness, lawful authority, reasons, accessible review, disability inclusion, privacy, transparency, safeguarding, accountability and effective service.

  • Procedural fairness. A person affected by a decision should generally know the case, have a real opportunity to respond, and receive reasons and review information where required. What issue was put to me? What opportunity did I have to answer it? Were reasons and review rights given?
  • Lawful authority. A decision-maker should identify the power being exercised and apply the correct legal test to the facts. What power was used? What test was applied? Were the relevant facts considered?
  • Reasons and transparency. A decision should explain what was decided, why, on what evidence, and what alternatives were considered. What reasons were given? What evidence was relied on? What alternatives were rejected, and why?
  • Accessible review. A review or complaint pathway should be usable by the person affected, not merely technically available. Could I realistically use the review offered, given my circumstances, resources, disability, communication restrictions or lack of representation?
  • No transfer without handover. A referral should transfer the issue with enough information for the receiving body to act; it should not simply move the person on. What information was sent? Did the next body accept it? What happened to the unresolved part?
  • Disability inclusion. Services and decisions should recognise disability-related barriers, communication needs, independence, safety and participation. Were my disability-related circumstances identified? Were access barriers addressed? Was independence considered before decisions such as vehicle disposal?
  • Safeguarding. Bodies with protective functions should act on risk, deterioration and reported harm rather than treating them as outside scope. What risk was reported? What was assessed? What protective action followed?
  • Privacy and records. Agencies should search records properly, explain what was found or not found, and give reasons for disclosure decisions. What was searched? What was located? Why was anything withheld, delayed or later released?
  • Accountability for cumulative impact. Each body should consider the consequences of its decisions within its knowledge and authority, especially where it knows the person is being passed between systems. Did anyone assess the combined effect? Who took responsibility for the person left behind?
  • Effective assistance. Assistance should produce a practical change in the person’s circumstances, not merely create a record of contact. What changed? What needs remained? What would have made the assistance effective?
  • Duty to act on notice. Where an institution receives information indicating risk, harm, deterioration or unmet need, it should record, assess and respond to that information. What notice was received? When? Who received it? What assessment followed? What action or omission resulted?
  • No procedural erasure. A process cannot erase the human consequences of the process. Closure, jurisdiction and workload management do not answer the substantive question of what happened to the person. What human consequence followed? Who considered it? What repair, support or change followed?
