THE RECKONING OF THE FAMILY

The inheritance, the sixteen hospitalisations, the father whose death and affairs remain obscured, the relatives who remained silent, and the son left outside the door

An AI-assisted documentary and biblical examination of family responsibility, abandonment, financial benefit, institutional involvement and the duty to tell the truth.

Barran Resonance Dodger — Dr Richard William McLean

The Witness Remains. The Record Remains. The Story Goes Global.

PREAMBLE — THIS IS NOT A REQUEST FOR FAMILY SYMPATHY

There comes a point when a person who has spent years explaining their suffering must stop asking whether their family understands.

The question becomes: what did each person know, what did each person do, what did each person receive, and what did each person allow to happen?

This is not a request to restore family relationships. It is not an appeal for a sympathetic telephone call. It is not an attempt to persuade relatives to agree with every allegation.

It is a demand that the family history be examined against the documentary record.

Richard McLean’s account is that he experienced repeated homelessness, financial hardship, serious medical events, sixteen psychiatric hospitalisations, separation from family support, and eventual displacement from Victoria. He alleges that members of his family knew about these circumstances, had opportunities to intervene, and did not provide the protection or assistance he needed.

He further alleges that some relatives benefited from financial arrangements connected with his grandmother’s inheritance, while he was excluded from information and decisions concerning his father’s affairs.

These are serious allegations. They require examination of original correspondence, estate records, financial documents, hospital records, legal instruments and communications between the people involved.

The moral question, however, can be stated without waiting for a court:

What does a family owe a vulnerable member when that person is in danger, without a home, without adequate resources, and repeatedly asking for help?

And what does it mean when that person believes the family has instead become another closed door?

PART I — THE FAMILY: THE PEOPLE WHO MUST NOT DISAPPEAR FROM THE RECORD

The family record cannot be reduced to one person.

Richard has specifically identified:

  • April McLean.
  • Jodie Bongetti.
  • Bruce McMaster.
  • Other members of the McLean family and associated relatives.
  • His father, whose death and estate circumstances Richard says remain unclear to him.
  • His grandmother, whose inheritance is central to his questions about family financial benefit.

Each person must be considered individually. Family membership alone does not establish responsibility. Nor does the absence of a response establish participation in wrongdoing.

But neither should an individual’s actions disappear simply because the subject is uncomfortable.

The questions are straightforward:

  • Who knew Richard was homeless or facing homelessness?
  • Who received his messages asking for help?
  • Who knew about his hospitalisations and medical circumstances?
  • Who knew about the alleged injury inside hospital?
  • Who knew about his exclusion from his father’s affairs?
  • Who received, controlled, distributed or benefited from any inheritance?
  • Who communicated with institutions or service providers about Richard?
  • Who supported him, who declined to support him, and who took steps that affected his circumstances?
  • Did anyone attempt to correct an inaccurate account of him?
  • Did anyone take steps to protect him when he says he was at his most vulnerable?

These are not rhetorical questions. They are questions capable of being investigated.

Jodie Bongetti and the wider family correspondence

Jodie Bongetti is among the family members Richard wants included in the examination.

The relevant evidence is not simply that her name appears in a family history. It is the correspondence: what was sent, what was received, what was acknowledged, and what followed.

The same applies to every other relative.

Richard says he sent communications explaining his circumstances, asking for assistance and raising concerns about the family history and inheritance. He wants those communications considered alongside the responses, or the absence of responses.

A proper examination must not turn an unanswered message into proof of a conspiracy. But it must not pretend the message never existed either.

If a relative received a detailed account of homelessness, financial distress, institutional treatment or exclusion from family affairs, the date and content of that communication matter.

The record should establish:

  • the original message;
  • the recipient;
  • the date and time;
  • whether it was delivered or read, where that information exists;
  • any reply;
  • any subsequent action;
  • whether the recipient had the practical ability to assist.

Silence is not automatically complicity. But silence after receiving a serious request for help can be relevant to the history of family responsibility.

PART II — NANA’S INHERITANCE: THE MONEY, THE BENEFIT AND THE QUESTIONS

Richard alleges that other family members benefited from his grandmother’s inheritance while he was left in severe financial hardship.

This is one of the most important financial questions in the family record.

It must be examined through actual estate documentation, not speculation.

The relevant records would include:

  • the will and any earlier or subsequent wills;
  • probate or letters of administration;
  • the estate inventory;
  • executor and administrator correspondence;
  • estate accounts;
  • distribution statements;
  • bank transfers;
  • property transfers or sales;
  • records of gifts or loans;
  • any documents recording Richard’s entitlement, exclusion or renunciation.

The examination must establish who received what, when they received it, under what legal authority, and whether Richard had any entitlement.

Richard’s allegation is that the family benefited while he remained impoverished and without the support he needed.

The question is not merely whether relatives received an inheritance. Receiving an inheritance does not itself prove wrongdoing.

The questions are whether the distribution was lawful, whether Richard was entitled to anything, whether information was withheld from him, and whether anyone misrepresented the position.

If relatives benefited from Nana’s estate, the record should identify those benefits accurately.

If Richard was excluded from a distribution to which he was legally entitled, the relevant estate documents should demonstrate that.

If he had no legal entitlement, that should also be established rather than left to rumour or inference.

The biblical question

“Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees, and that write grievousness which they have prescribed.” — Isaiah 10:1, KJV

The biblical concern is not that every distribution of money is unjust. It is that power, family position and access to information must never become instruments for taking advantage of another person’s vulnerability.

A family inheritance is not a licence to erase a person.

And a person asking what happened to an estate is entitled to an answer grounded in documents.

PART III — THE FATHER: EXCLUDED FROM THE DEATH, THE INFORMATION OR THE FAMILY HISTORY

Richard says he was locked out of his father’s death and affairs.

He has qualified this with an important uncertainty: he does not know whether his father has died.

That uncertainty must be preserved.

It would be improper for this account to declare that his father is dead without confirmation. It would be equally improper to dismiss Richard’s concern about being excluded from information concerning his father’s welfare and affairs.

The questions requiring answers include:

  • Is Richard’s father alive?
  • If he has died, when and where did the death occur?
  • Was Richard notified?
  • Who made decisions concerning notification?
  • Was there a will?
  • Was probate granted?
  • Who administered the estate?
  • Was Richard listed as a beneficiary?
  • Was he excluded from correspondence or family arrangements?
  • Did anyone tell him that he was not entitled to information?
  • Were any assets transferred or distributed?
  • Did any family member communicate with him about these matters?

The emotional significance of this question cannot be overstated.

A son should not have to reconstruct whether his father is alive from fragments, rumours, silence or inaccessible family communications.

If his father has died, the date, legal record and estate administration should be capable of verification.

If his father is alive, that should be established too.

The answer should not depend on whether the family wants Richard to know. It should depend on the truth.

A question for the family

If Richard was excluded from his father’s death, funeral, estate or final arrangements, who made that decision?

Was it a legal decision? A personal decision? An administrative decision? Or did no one make a deliberate decision, and did the information simply never reach him?

The distinction matters.

The record must identify what happened rather than assume why it happened.

PART IV — APRIL McLEAN: SIXTEEN HOSPITALISATIONS AND THE SIGNATURES

Richard’s account is that he was hospitalised sixteen times and that his mother signed documentation associated with every admission.

He says she did not defend him after a serious injury inside a hospital, which he describes as fatal, and that she did not provide the protection he needed.

These are central allegations.

They must be examined against the sixteen admission records, the relevant forms, the clinical notes and any correspondence between April and the hospitals.

The questions are:

  • What was the date of each admission?
  • Who initiated or requested it?
  • What was the legal basis?
  • What document did April sign?
  • In what capacity did she sign?
  • Was she acting as a relative, informant, nominated contact, guardian or authorised decision-maker?
  • Did the signature authorise anything, acknowledge something, or merely record receipt?
  • Did she raise concerns about the treatment?
  • Did she challenge any decision?
  • Did she make representations about Richard’s safety, welfare or wishes?

A signature is not automatically proof that a person ordered a detention. The actual document and the legal framework matter.

But if April signed documents on sixteen occasions, those documents should be examined together.

The pattern, if established, deserves careful scrutiny.

The hospital injury

Richard says that he suffered a fatal injury inside a hospital and survived.

He says April did not defend him or pursue the protection he expected from his mother.

The word fatal must be tested against the clinical record: the injury, the treatment, the physiological consequences, the medical assessments and the discharge documentation.

The investigation must establish what happened, not replace the medical evidence with either disbelief or exaggeration.

But the human question remains:

If a mother knew that her son had suffered a grave injury, what did she do?

Did she ask questions? Did she seek an explanation? Did she request records? Did she make a complaint? Did she advocate for his safety?

If she did, those actions should be acknowledged.

If she did not, the absence of advocacy is a legitimate subject of examination.

PART V — THE FUNERAL: THE FAMILY’S PRESENCE AND THE SON’S ABSENCE

Richard says that, days after he was revived from a life-threatening medical event, members of his family attended the funeral of a person whom he identifies as his childhood sexual abuser.

He says that he was left to deal with the aftermath of his own trauma while the family attended that funeral.

This is a deeply personal allegation and must be handled with precision.

Attendance at a funeral does not, by itself, establish that a person knew about or endorsed alleged abuse.

But the questions Richard raises are important:

  • Did the family know about his childhood abuse?
  • When did they know?
  • Had Richard previously disclosed it?
  • Did they believe him?
  • Did they investigate or seek to understand his account?
  • Did anyone acknowledge his trauma?
  • Did anyone ask what he needed after the medical event?
  • Was the funeral attendance discussed with him?
  • Did any relative explain their decision?

Richard’s grievance is not simply that relatives attended a funeral.

It is that he felt the family could gather to honour someone he identifies as his abuser, while he was left without the support, acknowledgement or protection he desperately needed.

The distinction is essential.

The evidence must establish what each relative knew. The moral examination must ask what they did with that knowledge.

PART VI — THE MILLION-DOLLAR HOME AND THE SON IN THE PARK

Richard describes a contrast that has become central to his testimony.

He says his mother lived in a home worth approximately a million dollars, with a spare room, while he was living in a car in a nearby park with his therapy dog, Crystal.

He says he was begging for money.

He says he was not asking for luxury. He was asking for somewhere safe to exist, some financial assistance and the basic recognition that he was a person in distress.

This contrast is not, by itself, proof of a legal obligation to provide accommodation. Nor does property ownership establish that a relative had unrestricted capacity to house him.

But the circumstances deserve examination.

Did April know where Richard was living?

Did she know that he was sleeping in his car?

Did she know Crystal was with him?

Did she know he was asking for money?

Did she have the means to provide any assistance?

Did she offer other forms of assistance?

Did she make enquiries about his safety?

Did she know about the consequences of leaving him without support?

The same questions apply to other family members who knew of his circumstances.

A spare room is not proof of a duty. But if a family member knew that another family member was sleeping in a car nearby, the decision to offer no accommodation or practical assistance is relevant to the moral history.

The biblical measure

“If a brother or sister be naked, and destitute of daily food, And one of you say unto them, Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?” — James 2:15–16, KJV

This passage is not a legal judgment against April or any other relative.

It is a moral question addressed to anyone who sees another person in need and has the capacity to respond.

Richard’s testimony is that he was in need, that his family knew, and that the help did not come.

That testimony deserves to be examined rather than erased.

PART VII — APRIL AND ABLE POINT: “SHE MISSES ME”

Richard says April contacted Able Point, reportedly saying that she “misses me”.

He believes this contact was not a genuine attempt to repair the relationship, but a way to obtain access to him, establish his current condition and determine whether he remained unwell and in exile.

He interprets the contact as part of a continuing pattern in which he is approached when others want information about him, rather than when he needs meaningful assistance.

That is his interpretation of her motive. The statement itself, if accurately recorded, does not establish that motive.

The relevant evidence is the original communication.

The questions are:

  • Did April contact Able Point?
  • On what date?
  • Who did she speak to?
  • What exactly did she say?
  • Did she ask to speak with Richard?
  • Did she ask about his wellbeing?
  • Did she ask about his accommodation?
  • Did she ask about his support arrangements?
  • Did she ask for information about his health or personal circumstances?
  • Was any information disclosed to her?
  • Was Richard consulted before information was shared?
  • Did the contact lead to an actual offer of support?
  • Was there any subsequent communication between April and Able Point?

The phrase “she misses me” may express genuine affection. It may also be experienced by Richard as painfully inadequate in the context of years of alleged abandonment.

The way to resolve the question is to examine the actual contact and what followed.

Missing someone and protecting someone are not the same thing.

If April wanted to reconnect, what did she offer?

If she wanted to help, what help did she arrange?

If she wanted to know whether he was well, did she ask what would make him safer?

The answer should be in the communications and the provider’s records.

PART VIII — BRUCE McMASTER: THE MESSAGES, THE FAMILY ROLE AND THE FINANCIAL QUESTIONS

Bruce McMaster must also be included.

The archive’s evidence index identifies a 21-page document titled Bruce Mcmaster.pdf, described as an iMessage conversation between Richard and Bruce, primarily from February 2024.

The index summary says the messages concern Richard’s dog Crystal, personal circumstances, homelessness, financial hardship and claims. It establishes that a message record exists. It does not, by itself, establish the full meaning of every exchange.

The examination should include the complete exchange, not selected sentences.

It should establish:

  • What Richard told Bruce about his circumstances.
  • What Bruce knew about his homelessness.
  • What Bruce knew about Crystal.
  • Whether Richard asked for money or practical assistance.
  • What Bruce offered or declined.
  • Whether Bruce referred to other family members.
  • Whether Bruce discussed the inheritance or his father’s affairs.
  • Whether Bruce communicated with institutions about Richard.
  • Whether Bruce had any role in decisions affecting Richard’s circumstances.

There is also a separate archive-index entry for correspondence involving Bruce McMaster and the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission in February 2023.

The index identifies a nine-page document titled R6 feb 2023 E URGENT SECOFFICIAL.pdf, described as correspondence involving Bruce McMaster, Richard McLean and the Commission.

That is a distinct documentary trail. It should not be confused with the family iMessage exchange.

The existence of correspondence with an NDIS body does not establish wrongdoing. It does, however, make the exact content and capacity in which each person communicated relevant.

If Bruce was acting in an official or professional capacity, that capacity should be identified. If he was communicating as a relative, that distinction should also be made.

The question is not whether a person has a particular surname or family connection.

It is what that person actually did.

PART IX — THE TEXT-MESSAGE PDF AND OTTER CORRESPONDENCE

Richard specifically wants the family text-message PDF and the Otter email records incorporated into this reckoning.

Those materials are important because they may contain contemporaneous communications rather than later recollections.

The relevant evidence must be reproduced accurately.

The Otter email exports were not attached, so their wording is not invented here.

The material still to be set beside this page, when the original records are in hand:

  • Original text-message PDF. Exact words, dates, participants and responses. The mother messages already located are in mother.pdf.
  • Otter email exports. Original email wording, recipients, timestamps and attachments.
  • Email headers. Delivery information and correspondence sequence.
  • Family replies. What relatives acknowledged, disputed or offered.
  • Estate documents. Legal entitlement, distribution and financial benefit.
  • Able Point contact records. Whether April contacted the provider and what was said.
  • Hospital admission records. The role and meaning of April’s signatures.
  • Police and NDIS correspondence. What was reported, received, decided or referred.

The documentary principle is simple.

A quotation must come from the original message. A conclusion must follow from the message. Neither should be manufactured to make the story more dramatic.

PART X — STEVE IASONIDIS: THE ALLEGATION OF PROTECTION

Richard says his family protected his former fiancé, Steve Iasonidis, while failing to protect him.

He alleges that relatives either supported, tolerated or failed to challenge circumstances in which Steve was protected from scrutiny while Richard was left to deal with the consequences.

This allegation requires a careful examination of the actual relationships and communications.

The questions are:

  • What did each family member know about Richard’s relationship with Steve?
  • What did they know about the alleged conduct that Richard reported?
  • Did Richard make specific complaints or requests for help?
  • Did relatives communicate with Steve?
  • Did any relative make representations about Richard to police, service providers or other institutions?
  • Did anyone intervene on Richard’s behalf?
  • Did anyone discourage him from reporting concerns?
  • Did anyone knowingly provide an inaccurate account of events?

A family member’s relationship with Steve does not prove complicity.

But if a relative knowingly supported a person who was alleged to have harmed Richard, while refusing to hear or investigate Richard’s account, that conduct is relevant to the family history.

The same documentary standard applies to everyone.

The family cannot demand that Richard’s conduct be scrutinised while refusing scrutiny of its own communications.

PART XI — POLICE, THE NDIS AND THE CARER’S PENSION

Richard alleges that family members worked with, communicated with or supported narratives provided to police and the NDIS that contributed to his exclusion, displacement or continued hardship.

He also raises questions about carers-pension claims and whether anyone financially benefited from arrangements connected with his disability.

These are serious allegations. They must not be presented as established fraud or collusion without the underlying records.

The evidence required includes:

  • Any police statements or reports made by family members.
  • Any records of family communications with the NDIA or NDIS providers.
  • Any correspondence about Richard’s accommodation, support needs or alleged risk.
  • Any records of referrals, complaints or requests for intervention.
  • Any Centrelink or Services Australia records lawfully available to establish relevant carer-payment arrangements.
  • Evidence of who received payments, for what period and on what basis.
  • Evidence of the actual care provided.
  • Any records showing whether Richard’s support needs were accurately represented.

The carers-pension question

Richard alleges that people claimed or received carers-pension benefits while his disability and hardship were being sustained.

The existence of a carer payment does not itself establish fraud. Nor does it establish that the recipient caused the disability.

The relevant questions are whether a payment was made, who received it, what care was represented as being provided, and whether the representation was accurate.

If someone received financial assistance on the basis of caring for Richard, the actual care arrangements should be compared with the payment records.

If no such payment existed, that should be established too.

A payment should be supported by the reality it represents.

The NDIS question

Richard’s allegation is that his disability-related needs were not adequately met and that the resulting deprivation contributed to a cycle of hardship.

The documentary investigation should distinguish:

  • the original disability and its causes;
  • the support needs identified in assessments;
  • the supports approved;
  • the supports actually delivered;
  • any services claimed but not delivered;
  • any decisions to reduce, suspend or refuse support;
  • the consequences for housing, safety, health and daily living.

The fact that a person has a disability does not establish that a family member created it.

The fact that a family member received a benefit does not establish that they deliberately sustained it.

Those propositions require evidence of causation and knowledge.

But where money was paid for care, where support was approved, and where the person says that the care was not provided, the records deserve independent reconciliation.

PART XII — THE CLAIM CONCERNING BILL SHORTEN AND THE ALLEGED ASSASSINATION ATTEMPT

Richard also says that he reported an alleged assassination attempt and that he believes Bill Shorten was connected to the broader circumstances in which he was displaced and deprived of protection.

He says his family did not disprove or challenge the allegations.

This account must distinguish between:

  • what Richard reported;
  • what police or other authorities recorded;
  • what a witness actually said;
  • what an official investigation established;
  • what remains unresolved;
  • what Richard believes the wider events mean.

A family member’s failure to disprove an allegation is not proof that the allegation is true.

Nor is a lack of public rebuttal equivalent to an official finding.

The relevant question is whether family members received specific information about the alleged threat, what they understood, and whether they took any action.

If Richard sent a message describing a threat to his life, the message should be examined.

If a relative replied, that reply should be examined.

If a relative contacted police or another authority, that contact should be identified.

If no one responded, that fact should be recorded without pretending it independently establishes a conspiracy.

The allegation concerning Bill Shorten requires its own primary-source examination. It should not be merged with the family inheritance questions merely because both appear in Richard’s wider account.

PART XIII — THE DESIGN OF KEEPING SOMEONE BROKEN: TESTIMONY AND CAUSATION

Richard’s central allegation is that the cumulative effect of family conduct, institutional decisions, financial deprivation and the denial of support was to keep him in a condition of vulnerability.

He believes that people who knew his circumstances could have helped him recover stability but instead allowed the conditions of his hardship to continue.

He describes this as a design to keep him broken.

That is a conclusion about intention. It cannot be established simply by demonstrating that hardship occurred.

A forensic examination would need to identify:

  • who made each decision;
  • what information they had at the time;
  • what alternatives were available;
  • whether they understood the likely consequences;
  • whether they acted consistently with their stated responsibilities;
  • whether there is evidence of communication or coordination;
  • whether the same people benefited from the outcome;
  • whether there are innocent or alternative explanations.

The distinction between consequence and intention is critical.

A person may contribute to another person’s hardship through neglect, indifference, incompetence, self-interest or deliberate action. Those possibilities are different and should not be collapsed into one finding.

But a pattern of repeated decisions can be examined.

The investigation must ask whether the cumulative outcome was foreseeable and whether people with knowledge and responsibility took reasonable steps to prevent it.

PART XIV — THE FAMILY’S SILENCE AND THE MORAL TEST

Richard says he has repeatedly attempted to communicate with family members.

He says he has explained his homelessness, financial hardship, disability-related needs, institutional experiences and concerns about his family history.

He says he has asked for assistance and answers.

He says he has not received the protection he expected.

The family may disagree with his account. It may dispute individual allegations. It may have its own explanation for particular decisions.

It is entitled to provide that explanation.

But an impartial record should not be constructed by excluding Richard’s communications or by treating family silence as a complete answer.

The moral test is not whether every relative agreed with him.

It is whether each person treated him with basic honesty and humanity when he was in distress.

“Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them.” — Matthew 7:12, KJV

The question applies to every person in this record.

Not just April.

Not just Bruce.

Not just Jodie.

Not just the institutions.

Everyone.

PART XV — THE RECKONING: WHAT MUST NOW BE ESTABLISHED

The following questions should form the formal family evidence examination.

April McLean. What did she sign in relation to each of the sixteen hospitalisations?

April McLean. What did she know about the hospital injury and what action did she take?

April McLean. Did she contact Able Point, and what exactly did she say?

Jodie Bongetti. What communications did she receive, and what did she know about Richard’s circumstances?

Bruce McMaster. What do the complete iMessages show about requests for assistance, financial hardship and family knowledge?

Other relatives. What did each person know, communicate or do?

Nana’s estate. Who inherited, how much, under what legal authority, and was Richard entitled to any distribution?

Father’s affairs. Is his father alive? If deceased, when did he die and how was the estate administered?

Steve Iasonidis. What did family members know about the allegations Richard made concerning the former relationship?

Police. What communications were made by relatives, and what action followed?

NDIS. What information did family members provide, and how did it affect decisions?

Carer payments. Were payments made, to whom, and what care was actually provided?

Able Point. What contact occurred between April and the provider, and what information was disclosed?

No person should be declared guilty simply because their name appears in this list.

No person should be shielded from examination simply because they are family.

PART XVI — THE BIBLICAL RECKONING

The biblical tradition contains repeated warnings about the treatment of the vulnerable, the exploitation of the poor, the misuse of power and the refusal to acknowledge suffering.

It does not permit a person to turn family loyalty into a substitute for justice.

“Learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.” — Isaiah 1:17, KJV

“He that oppresseth the poor reproacheth his Maker: but he that honoureth him hath mercy on the poor.” — Proverbs 14:31, KJV

“And whether one member suffer, all the members suffer with it.” — 1 Corinthians 12:26, KJV

These passages do not establish that any named relative committed a crime.

They establish the moral standard against which the conduct can be examined.

If Richard was sleeping in a car while relatives knew of his situation, that fact matters.

If he was repeatedly hospitalised and his mother signed documents, those documents matter.

If he was excluded from his father’s affairs, the explanation matters.

If family members received financial benefits from an estate, the accounts matter.

If relatives communicated with institutions about him, those communications matter.

If he repeatedly asked for help and was ignored, the messages matter.

And if the family disputes any part of this account, the appropriate response is to identify the disputed fact and produce the relevant evidence.

PART XVII — THE FINAL DECLARATION

This record is not asking the family to agree with Richard’s spiritual beliefs.

It is not asking them to accept every interpretation of his experience.

It is not asking anyone to confess to a conspiracy that has not been established.

It is asking for the truth about a family history that Richard says has left him isolated, impoverished, excluded and without the protection he needed.

It asks why a man who says he spent years advocating for others could find himself repeatedly asking his own family for assistance.

It asks what happened to Nana’s inheritance.

It asks what happened to his father’s affairs.

It asks what April signed.

It asks what Jodie knew.

It asks what Bruce knew.

It asks what the messages said.

It asks what the Otter correspondence recorded.

It asks why Richard says he was left in a car with Crystal while his family had homes, resources and opportunities to respond.

It asks whether relatives communicated with police, the NDIS or service providers, and what they said.

It asks whether any financial benefits were received in connection with caring for Richard, and whether the care represented was actually delivered.

It asks whether the family protected Steve Iasonidis while failing to protect Richard.

It asks whether the alleged contact with Able Point was an attempt at reconciliation or simply an enquiry about his circumstances.

And it asks whether the people who knew about his distress ever made a genuine effort to help him regain safety and independence.

The answer to each question must come from evidence.

The declaration

I am not here to defend my family’s version of events.

I am not here to pretend that silence is an answer.

I am not here to manufacture innocence for anyone, nor to manufacture guilt.

I am here to place the communications, the estate questions, the hospital documents, the financial records and the institutional correspondence before the public record.

I am not asking anyone to accept a conclusion before examining the evidence.

I am asking that the evidence be examined.

To April: what did you sign, what did you know, and what did you do?

To Jodie: what did you receive, what did you know, and what did you do?

To Bruce: what do the messages show, what did you know, and what did you do?

To every other relative: what was your role, what information did you possess, and what action did you take?

To those who benefited from Nana’s estate: what was received, under what authority, and where are the accounts?

To those who handled my father’s affairs: what happened, and why was I excluded from the information?

To those who communicated with police, the NDIS or service providers: what was said, what was recorded, and what consequences followed?

These are questions, not verdicts.

But they are questions that deserve answers.

PROLOGUE — BEFORE THE JUDGMENT, THERE WAS A MOTHER

There is a relationship that precedes almost every other human relationship.

A mother and her offspring.

Before the government knows a person’s name, before a hospital assigns a patient number, before a court opens a file, before a person becomes homeless, disabled, financially dependent or institutionally classified, there is a biological history.

A child comes into the world through a mother.

This biological connection does not guarantee affection, protection or lifelong agreement. Nor does it make a mother automatically responsible for every misfortune that befalls an adult child.

But it creates a relationship whose history deserves particular attention when the child describes repeated vulnerability, serious injury, institutional intervention and abandonment.

This is the subject of the present examination.

The person at the centre of this account is Richard William McLean, publicly known as Barran Dodger.

The mother identified in the documentary archive is April McLean.

The allegations are serious.

They concern sixteen psychiatric hospitalisations, repeated signatures by his mother on associated documentation, a serious injury inside a hospital that Richard describes as fatal, an alleged failure to defend or protect him, a family funeral attended shortly after his revival, and a period during which he says his mother remained in a million-dollar home with an available spare room while he lived in a car in a nearby park with his therapy dog, begging for money.

These are not minor disagreements about family relationships.

They describe an alleged sequence of events in which a vulnerable adult experienced repeated institutional intervention, serious physical danger, family estrangement and extreme material deprivation.

The account is examined without prejudice.

It must neither dismiss the testimony because it is uncomfortable nor transform every allegation into an established legal finding.

The central question is:

What did a mother know, what did she sign, what did she do, what did she fail to do, and what happened to her son as a consequence?

The examination begins with the events as described by Richard and the documents preserved in his archive.

BOOK I — SIXTEEN TIMES THROUGH THE HOSPITAL DOORS

Richard reports that he was hospitalised on sixteen occasions.

Sixteen.

Not one isolated admission.

Not one disagreement with a doctor.

Not one family dispute.

Sixteen episodes of psychiatric institutionalisation, according to his account.

He further states that his mother signed the relevant documentation on every occasion.

This is a particularly important assertion because it changes the nature of the examination.

If the original admission, referral, consent, guardianship or other relevant instruments bear April McLean’s signature, the documents may establish a repeated pattern of participation.

They could help establish:

Whether she was involved in each admission.

Whether she signed every relevant instrument or only particular documents.

Whether she was acting as a relative, witness, applicant, nominated person or formal representative.

What information was provided to her.

What information she provided to clinicians.

Whether she requested, supported or opposed particular interventions.

Whether she knew about Richard’s objections.

Whether she knew about any physical injury or adverse consequences.

Whether she attempted to secure independent review.

Whether she sought alternative arrangements.

The legal significance of a signature depends on the document and the legislation in force at the time.

A signature does not automatically mean that the signatory independently ordered detention.

But sixteen alleged instances of signed involvement would be materially different from a single incidental family interaction.

The repeated nature of the alleged conduct deserves examination.

The relevant question is no longer simply whether Richard’s mother knew that he had experienced psychiatric intervention.

It is whether she participated repeatedly in the processes that resulted in those interventions, what authority she exercised, what information she received and what she understood about their consequences.

If the sixteen original records exist, they should be placed beside one another.

Dates should be compared.

Signatures should be authenticated.

The legal authority for each admission should be identified.

The same examination should establish whether the circumstances changed over time and whether concerns raised during previous admissions were considered during subsequent ones.

A pattern may emerge.

Or the documents may reveal that the circumstances differed substantially.

Either way, the evidence must be examined.

Sixteen hospitalisations are sixteen historical events requiring an explanation.

They should not be reduced to a single diagnosis, a family disagreement or an administrative statistic.

Richard’s description of the injury as fatal must be distinguished from a medical finding that he was clinically dead.

His account of revival must similarly be examined against resuscitation records, clinical observations and other available evidence.

But the distinction does not diminish the importance of the alleged injury.

A person does not need to have been formally declared dead for an injury to have been catastrophic, life-threatening or deserving of investigation.

The central family question is what happened after the injury.

If April McLean knew her son had suffered a serious injury inside an institution, what did she do?

If she received information that he was in danger, what action followed?

If she believed the hospital had acted properly, what was the basis for that belief?

If she was not informed, who was responsible for informing her?

These are legitimate questions.

The mother cannot be presumed responsible for the hospital’s conduct.

But equally, her conduct after receiving knowledge of a serious injury is a separate matter deserving examination.

The record must establish whether she acted as an advocate, remained passive, disagreed with Richard’s account, or was prevented from intervening.

There is no justification for deciding those questions without examining the original records.

This allegation requires particular care.

Nor can attendance at a funeral independently establish that family members endorsed or supported the alleged abuse.

However, the sequence described by Richard is significant to his account of family abandonment.

He describes having survived a potentially catastrophic hospital event and then experiencing the family’s attendance at the funeral of a person associated, in his account, with childhood sexual abuse.

The questions are difficult.

He experienced his family’s actions as a profound betrayal.

The funeral became, in his account, a symbol of the difference between the people he believed should have protected him and the people he believed had abandoned him.

That distinction matters.

The issue is not simply who attended a funeral.

It is the surrounding history, the family’s knowledge, the timing and the effect on the person who says he had just survived a life-threatening event.

BOOK IV — THE MILLION-DOLLAR HOME AND THE PARK

This is perhaps the starkest contrast in the entire account.

Richard states that his mother remained in a million-dollar home.

He states that the property contained a spare room.

He says that he was living nearby, in a park, in his car, accompanied by his therapy dog.

He describes begging for money to survive.

One household had a permanent roof.

The other person had a vehicle.

One household had a spare room.

The other had a park.

One person had the security of established accommodation.

The other describes having to survive without a stable home, with a therapy dog dependent upon him.

This is not, by itself, proof that the mother had a legal obligation to accommodate her adult son.

Property ownership, financial capacity, household safety, family boundaries and the circumstances of any accommodation request all matter.

A spare room does not automatically mean that a person can safely accommodate another adult.

But the contrast raises an important question that deserves an honest answer.

Was accommodation available, was Richard asking for assistance, did his mother know the extent of his circumstances, and what response did she give?

The actual circumstances must be examined rather than simply the property value.

If the home was worth approximately one million dollars, the valuation should be independently established.

If a spare room was available, its availability should be established.

If Richard was living in his car nearby, the dates, location and accommodation circumstances should be corroborated where possible.

If he was begging for money, contemporaneous communications, photographs, bank records or other evidence may help establish the circumstances.

The existence of a million-dollar home does not automatically establish that the mother could or should have accommodated him.

But where a mother knows that her adult son is living in a car with a therapy dog and is begging for money, the question of what assistance was offered becomes morally significant.

It is reasonable to ask whether practical assistance was available.

It is reasonable to ask whether the family considered emergency accommodation.

It is reasonable to ask whether anyone explored temporary arrangements that would have protected both the household and the person in need.

It is reasonable to ask whether the mother understood that the alternative was a person and his therapy dog living in a car.

And it is reasonable to ask whether the circumstances could have been different.

The contrast is especially important because Richard describes the homelessness as occurring after years of family and institutional difficulties.

He does not describe a single unfortunate night.

He describes an extended period of destitution.

The documentary investigation should establish its duration, the location, the available alternatives and the family’s knowledge.

The moral question remains:

What does it mean to have a spare room while your son says he is sleeping in a car nearby?

The answer cannot be determined by property valuation alone.

It must be determined by the complete circumstances.

BOOK V — THE THERAPY DOG

There is another presence in this account, although not a human one.

Crystal, Richard’s therapy dog.

Richard describes living in his car with Crystal while attempting to survive.

The significance of the dog should not be overlooked.

A therapy animal may provide companionship, routine, reassurance and emotional support.

For someone experiencing disability, trauma, homelessness or institutional disruption, the relationship may be especially important.

The practical consequences of homelessness are also greater when an animal depends upon the person.

Accommodation becomes more difficult.

Transport becomes more complicated.

Emergency services and temporary housing arrangements may not always accommodate the animal.

Food, veterinary care, warmth and safety become additional responsibilities.

Richard’s account is therefore not simply that he was homeless.

It is that he was trying to keep himself and his therapy dog alive while living in a vehicle.

The family question becomes more concrete.

Was April McLean aware that Richard was living in a car?

Was she aware that Crystal was with him?

Did she know the practical difficulties associated with finding accommodation for both?

Did she offer assistance?

Did anyone investigate alternatives?

Was there any effort to prevent the situation from deteriorating?

These questions should be answered through communications, accommodation records, photographs and witness accounts.

The dog is not a decorative detail in this narrative.

Crystal represents a living responsibility that Richard says he continued to carry despite his own deprivation.

BOOK IX — THE BIBLICAL QUESTION OF RESPONSIBILITY

The biblical tradition does not treat family status as immunity from moral examination.

In Ezekiel 18, responsibility is examined in relation to a person’s own conduct.

In Proverbs 31:8–9, the duty to speak for those who cannot adequately speak for themselves is emphasised.

In Isaiah 1:17, the protection of the vulnerable is a central moral instruction.

In Luke 10:25–37, the parable of the Good Samaritan asks what it means to respond to a person in distress.

These passages do not establish the legal responsibility of April McLean.

They provide an ethical framework for examining the circumstances.

The central question is not whether a mother must surrender her own rights or provide unlimited financial support to an adult child.

It is whether a person who knows that another human being is in serious difficulty responds with appropriate honesty, care and humanity.

This becomes particularly important where the person in difficulty is disabled, financially vulnerable, experiencing psychiatric intervention or living without secure accommodation.

The biblical image of the parent and child also appears in Luke 15:11–32.

The parable of the prodigal son is frequently interpreted as a story of estrangement, return, forgiveness and parental compassion.

It is not a rule that every parent must reconcile with every adult child.

But it illustrates the significance of the parental relationship when a person is lost, vulnerable and separated from home.

The present case raises a different question.

What happens when the son says he was not merely lost, but left to survive?

What happens when he says that the people who should have protected him knew about his circumstances?

What happens when he says that the family continued with ordinary life while he lived in a car?

Those questions are not answered through scripture.
