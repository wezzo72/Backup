The declaration

I refused to die in the ashes of human betrayal.

But I survived.

The darkness and the rebuilding

I looked directly into my own darkness. And I am not merely the person who survived. I am the person who continued to dream, who found the strength to heal, who rebuilt without the rescue that never came, and who still believes that a better life is possible.

I have known the darkness of betrayal, but I will not make betrayal my identity. I have experienced the consequences of abandonment, but I will not abandon myself. 3 November 2026 · Added to the SOS · Testimony · It stands without a lawyer’s permission

I want to leave, but I have been deprived of the means to escape.

I want to leave. I have repeatedly made that clear. But I have no car, no practical means of transport, and no financial capacity to recover the vehicle that was taken from me. The Public Guardian took my car, and I have been left without the very means I need to escape the circumstances in which I find myself.

The history of how I became trapped is important because it demonstrates the cumulative absurdity of what has happened. When I was hospitalised, I had no home through no fault of my own. I had been forced out of my home and made vulnerable. Instead of addressing the circumstances that had deprived me of housing, financial security and independence, the system placed me in hospital. I did not need to be deprived of my freedom and treated as though hospitalisation was the answer to my circumstances. I needed my compensation, financial security, a lawyer and assistance to restore my independence.

At one point, a car was purchased for me so that I could escape the circumstances in which I was living. Yet I was subsequently hospitalised, and my circumstances deteriorated further. The absurdity is that my Centrelink Disability Support Pension card carried the hospital as my residential address. Consider what that means: a person who had lost his home, been deprived of his financial independence and had his circumstances converted into a hospital admission was effectively recorded as residing in an institution rather than being assisted to recover a home and a life.

I needed a home. I needed my money. I needed legal representation. I needed the means to participate in society and defend my rights. Instead, I experienced a sequence of decisions that removed the practical means of achieving those things.

The financial consequences were particularly cruel. While I was in hospital, the financial guardianship arrangements required me to pay rent out of my pension. The very income that should have been protected and administered in my interests was being used to meet housing expenses while I was unable to occupy my home in the ordinary way. I ask what meaningful protection was being provided by a financial guardianship arrangement if its practical effect was to leave me without control over my money while continuing to impose financial obligations upon me. Guardianship is supposed to protect a person’s interests, not compound their vulnerability or make it harder for them to regain independence.

The injustice becomes even more significant when considered alongside the removal of my vehicle. The police permitted the government repossession of my car, as I understand the events, without any compensation being provided to me for the loss. I dispute the legitimacy and fairness of what occurred and demand an explanation of the authority under which the vehicle was taken, the process followed, the legal basis for the decision and the arrangements for compensation or return. I have no practical means of getting my car back, and no vehicle with which to leave.

This is not a minor inconvenience. It is a direct restriction on my ability to exercise freedom of movement, secure alternative accommodation, access services, obtain legal assistance and remove myself from an environment in which I have repeatedly said I do not wish to remain.

The financial guardianship arrangements and the Public Guardian’s involvement must also be examined alongside the restrictions I have encountered in accessing Legal Aid. The Public Guardian has not resolved the legal assistance problem. Instead, the existing Legal Aid restriction has been accepted as another administrative reality. The result, as I experience it, is inter-agency entrapment: one institution makes or maintains a decision, another accepts its consequences, and neither takes responsibility for ensuring that I have a practical way to challenge the decisions affecting my life.

This is how accountability disappears between agencies. Each institution can point to another institution’s decision, policy, authority or responsibility. Meanwhile, I am the person who lives with the consequences. I am left without my vehicle, without the financial independence necessary to organise my departure, and without the legal representation necessary to challenge the arrangements that have contributed to my circumstances.

I cannot reasonably be expected to possess the financial resources, physical capacity, transport, institutional access or legal assistance necessary to escape a situation when the very systems that are supposed to protect me have become part of the barriers I must overcome. It is an extraordinary contradiction to tell a person that they have rights while leaving them without the practical means to exercise those rights.

When you remove someone’s money, you do more than remove a financial resource. You remove the means by which that person participates in a democracy. Money is necessary to secure accommodation, obtain transport, engage a lawyer, purchase necessities and make independent decisions. When a person is deprived of those means, their formal rights can remain written on paper while their practical ability to exercise them is systematically diminished.

That is the significance of what I describe as the systemic removal of the attributes necessary to live with dignity in a democracy. Income, compensation, money, a home, safety, employment, human rights and legal rights are not isolated conveniences. They are interconnected conditions of independent existence. Remove enough of them, and a person can become trapped in circumstances from which they cannot reasonably escape.

Every person deserves a home. I have repeatedly sought the opportunity to leave and establish an independent life. Yet I feel as though I have been given a prison made not of walls and bars, but of bureaucracy, financial restrictions, institutional decisions, unanswered correspondence and agencies that refuse to take responsibility. I am not a criminal. I should not have to live as though I am serving a sentence imposed by an accumulation of administrative decisions that I cannot afford to challenge.

I also raise the significance of Ablepoint’s alleged refusal to report or appropriately escalate a death threat before the Wyong Court proceedings. This is a matter requiring a proper examination of the original communications, the circumstances in which the threat was raised, who received the information, what action was requested, what action was taken and why. If a provider entrusted with supporting and safeguarding a vulnerable participant receives information about a death threat, the response cannot simply be silence or administrative avoidance. The question of whether the threat was appropriately reported and escalated is fundamental to the assessment of safeguarding, duty of care and institutional accountability.

I have repeatedly raised concerns about my safety and circumstances. I have sought assistance. I have asked to leave. I have asked for responses. I have asked for accountability. Yet the CEO has refused to respond to me or provide a substantive answer to the concerns I have raised. Instead, I have encountered silence. That silence has become part of the problem because it prevents meaningful examination of the decisions, allegations and failures I am asking the organisations involved to address.

The financial guardianship arrangements are supposed to protect me. The disability support system is supposed to support me. The provider is supposed to safeguard me. The legal system is supposed to provide access to justice. The police are supposed to protect the public. Government agencies are supposed to administer their responsibilities lawfully and accountably. But what happens when the person who needs those protections experiences each institution as another obstacle to obtaining them?

I allege that the cumulative effect of these decisions and omissions has been to silence me as a whistleblower, undermine my independence and erase my ability to participate in ordinary life. I use the word erasure deliberately. A person does not have to be physically killed to experience the destruction of their public identity, economic independence, security, relationships and capacity to be heard. When a person’s body remains alive but the practical conditions necessary to live with dignity are systematically removed, the human consequences can be devastating.

I regard this as an issue of profound public importance. The allegations must be investigated through the original records, correspondence, financial transactions, guardianship decisions, police records, hospital records, Legal Aid decisions and provider communications. It is not sufficient for each agency to defend its own isolated decision without examining the cumulative consequences of those decisions.

I want to leave. I have said so. I have no car. I have no practical means of recovering the car that was taken. I have not received compensation for its loss. I have experienced restrictions on my access to money and legal assistance. I am asking for the means to regain my independence, not another explanation of why somebody else is responsible.

This is the corruption I am asking the public to examine: not merely an individual decision, but the possibility that multiple institutions can contribute to a person’s entrapment while each escapes responsibility for the overall outcome.

I will not accept silence as an adequate response. I will not accept the proposition that my circumstances are simply somebody else’s administrative problem. I will not accept being deprived of the means to leave and then being held responsible for remaining where I am.

They attempted to silence me. They attempted to erase me. But they failed to stop me from documenting what happened, preserving the evidence and asking the questions that remain unanswered.

The silence has broken. I am still here. And I want my freedom, my independence, my vehicle, my financial security, access to legal representation and the opportunity to live in a home of my own.

I am asking for assistance to leave, and I am asking for an independent investigation into how I came to be deprived of the means to do so.

One important distinction: I have framed the alleged unlawful repossession, institutional failures and deliberate silencing as allegations requiring investigation, rather than presenting disputed criminal conduct as already judicially established. That makes the SOS more defensible and harder to dismiss.
