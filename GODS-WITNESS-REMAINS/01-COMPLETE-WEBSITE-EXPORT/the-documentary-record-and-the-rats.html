THE WITNESS REMAINS. THE RECORD REMAINS. THE STORY GOES GLOBAL.

There are stories that begin with a person’s account of what happened to them. There are stories that begin with an official document. And there are stories that emerge when those two things are placed alongside one another, when correspondence is preserved, decisions are examined, dates are reconstructed, and a record that might otherwise have remained scattered becomes accessible to the public.

This declaration begins there.

Not with an assumption that every allegation has been established. Not with a predetermined verdict against any individual or institution. Not with a demand that the reader accept a spiritual interpretation as historical fact. It begins with the documentary record, the surviving correspondence, the administrative decisions, the public registers, the published material and the questions that remain capable of independent examination.

Its central declaration is that a human being’s account must not disappear simply because it is inconvenient, complicated, contested or difficult to investigate.

The story associated with Barran Resonance Dodger is now presented through two principal public repositories:

  • The documentary archive: Barrandodger — GitHub repository.
  • The prophetic and literary publication: God’s Witness Remains — The Great Reckoning.

These are the starting points for examination. Their contents should be read alongside the original issuing bodies’ records wherever those records are available. The existence of an archived document, the wording it contains, the action recorded in it and the interpretation subsequently placed upon it are distinct evidentiary matters. Each deserves to be examined on its own terms.

The declaration that follows uses the dramatic progression of the supplied YouTube transcript: survival, the construction of the cage, the documentary trail, the reversal of silence, the examination of identity, the confrontation with institutions, the return of the record and the final reckoning.

But the subject is different. This is not a declaration about an alleged FBI trafficking or experimentation programme. Those claims in the supplied video are not evidence about this Australian case. The following work concerns the actual documentary subjects identified in the archive and the public material, without importing another person’s allegations into this record.

PROLOGUE — THE MAN WHO WOULD NOT DISAPPEAR

There is a particular kind of institutional silence that cannot be understood by reading one letter in isolation. It emerges across years, through repeated correspondence, administrative decisions, changing reference numbers, requests for information, complaints, acknowledgments, refusals, reviews and the persistent effort of one person to establish what happened, who knew, what was decided and what remains outstanding.

In this record, the chronology reaches back at least to 2021 and extends into 2026.

It includes correspondence with the Australian Federal Police, the Commonwealth Ombudsman, Comcare, the Federal Court of Australia, the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, the National Disability Insurance Agency, the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission, the Australian Human Rights Commission and other public institutions.

It also includes a later chapter involving NSW Trustee & Guardian, supported accommodation, Able Point, disability services, financial independence, the vehicle decision and the importance of Crystal.

These are not interchangeable matters. Each has its own records, responsible organisations, dates, decisions and avenues of examination. Their cumulative significance lies in the possibility of reconstructing a person’s experience across institutional boundaries rather than allowing every event to be considered as though nothing happened before it.

The public archive contains a particularly important distinction: the difference between a document issued by an agency and a document written by the person seeking assistance.

The Master Evidence Index and Authoritative Finding Aid, dated 19 October 2026, explicitly organises the record into official records, allegations and inferences. It identifies a curated selection from a much larger documentary collection and gives readers named documents and source locations.

The Master Integrity and Source Map identifies a consolidated 148-page documentary compilation, its three source appendices, original document hashes and the precise page ranges occupied by those appendices. It also records the integrity limitations of the compilation.

The Master Narrative and Registers brings together correspondence, a chronological narrative, documentary registers, requests for assistance and source appendices.

These three records provide an essential starting point for anyone seeking to understand the documentary architecture.

And so the first declaration is made:

A person is more than the administrative description assigned to them. A life is more than the sum of its case numbers. And a documentary history deserves to be examined as a whole.

The man at the centre of this archive has described his circumstances as political exile within the democracy of his own citizenship. He has described institutional betrayal, loss of independence, barriers to protection and the experience of repeatedly seeking answers from organisations whose responsibilities overlap.

That description is his testimony.

The documentary investigation asks what the institutions recorded, what they answered, what they decided and what action followed.

The distinction is important because the story must be capable of surviving independent examination.

PART I — THEY THOUGHT THE RECORD WOULD END WITH THE CORRESPONDENCE

Chapter 1 — The first wall: when a human appeal becomes an administrative matter

The first major theme in the supplied documentary chronology is the attempt to obtain assistance and accountability through correspondence with Commonwealth institutions.

One of the most important source bundles is the original correspondence concerning the Prime Minister and Cabinet in May 2022.

The master compilation identifies an earlier communication dated 10 May 2022, sent to the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet’s Financial Governance Team.

The communication requested urgent acknowledgment and assistance. It described serious personal circumstances, including financial hardship, difficulties obtaining food and accommodation, illness and the need for practical support.

The language was urgent because the author was describing a situation he considered urgent.

On 13 May 2022, the PM&C Financial Governance Team responded. The reproduced correspondence acknowledged receipt of the 10 May communication and identified an apparent connection with a freedom-of-information request.

It also provided information about review and complaint pathways and offered a Compensation for Detriment caused by Defective Administration application form.

That exchange is significant for a straightforward documentary reason: the correspondence establishes a dated interaction between a person requesting assistance and a Commonwealth department responding through its administrative processes.

The later communication of 31 May addressed to the Prime Minister’s Office again described hardship and requested intervention.

The original bundle is particularly important because it allows the chronology to be read in sequence:

  • 10 May 2022 — initial urgent appeal and correspondence bundle.
  • 31 May 2022 — letter addressed to Prime Minister Anthony Albanese and OPMC.
  • The Sovereign Witness — consolidated documentary and humanitarian submission.
  • The Barran Dodger humanitarian appeal — source appendix.

The issue raised by this chronology is not simply whether an email was received.

It is whether the practical circumstances described in the correspondence were subsequently addressed, what assistance was offered, what assistance was delivered, what matters were referred elsewhere and whether the cumulative history was considered when later decisions were made.

There is a profound difference between providing a person with a pathway to submit another form and establishing whether the underlying human need has been met.

That difference is central to this declaration.

The record asks the reader to examine not merely the language of administration but the human consequences of administration.

Chapter 2 — The 1,187 Outlook items

A second documentary sequence concerns freedom-of-information request FOI/2022/045.

The correspondence reproduced in the master source material identifies an original request dated 7 February 2022.

The request sought information held about the applicant.

Subsequent correspondence refers to the difficulty of identifying relevant records and the need to distinguish personal information from information involving other individuals.

One communication from the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner refers to approximately 1,187 Outlook items being examined for relevance.

The later departmental decision, dated 14 April 2022, records a refusal under section 24A(1)(b) of the Freedom of Information Act.

The significance of the chronology is the sequence itself.

A broad request was made. Searches were undertaken. A substantial number of Outlook items were identified for examination. The department subsequently recorded that relevant documents could not be identified within the scope of the request.

The later humanitarian appeal identifies an additional revised decision dated 8 October 2024 concerning five documents.

The precise investigative questions are:

  • What searches were conducted?
  • What search terms and date ranges were used?
  • How were the Outlook items screened?
  • What was the relationship between the original request and the subsequent revised decision?
  • Which five documents were subsequently identified?
  • What did the revised decision change?
  • What records remain available for inspection?

These are questions that can be answered through the underlying administrative record.

The declaration therefore demands a complete documentary chronology rather than an isolated reading of the final letter.

The correspondence must be read in sequence. The decisions must be read in context. The record must remain accessible.

The archive contains a number of documents relating to Public Interest Disclosures, assessment processes and correspondence with government institutions.

A decision not to allocate a disclosure is an important administrative event in the chronology. The reasons for the decision, the statutory provision relied upon, the subject matter considered and any available review pathway deserve careful examination.

The larger question is how a person can navigate multiple disclosure pathways when the same underlying history involves several organisations.

That question extends beyond one applicant.

It concerns the accessibility of public-interest disclosure mechanisms, the protection of people who report perceived wrongdoing and the ability of institutions to provide intelligible explanations of their decisions.

Chapter 4 — The Australian Federal Police correspondence

The archive contains a dated Australian Federal Police submission record from 3 September 2021.

The archive also identifies correspondence relating to the Commonwealth Ombudsman and subsequent complaint processes.

The important forensic questions concern what was submitted, how the subject matter was described, what response was issued and what further options were provided.

An independent examination should distinguish the original submission from the agency’s response and from the interpretation later placed upon the exchange.

This matters because an individual’s description of an event, an agency’s receipt of that description and an agency’s assessment of its jurisdiction are three separate parts of a documentary history.

The declaration calls for those parts to be brought together.

The same principle applies to all other institutions.

No agency should be assigned responsibility for a decision that belongs to another. Equally, a person seeking assistance should not be left without a clear explanation of where responsibility lies.

PART II — THE DOCUMENTS THAT REMAIN

Chapter 5 — The Commonwealth Ombudsman and the unanswered questions

The archive identifies several Commonwealth Ombudsman documents, including a service restriction letter of 8 August 2025, and the 2023 and 2024 notifications and non-allocation correspondence already identified.

The sequence raises questions about the development of the relationship between the applicant and the Ombudsman, the nature of the matters submitted, the applicable complaint processes and the reasons for any restrictions placed on further contact.

An examination should identify:

  • The original complaints and their dates.
  • The statutory or administrative provisions relied upon.
  • The matters the Ombudsman considered.
  • The reasons recorded in each decision.
  • The communication arrangements subsequently imposed.
  • The available alternative avenues of review or complaint.

The archive also contains correspondence concerning a complaint response dated 25 September 2023, and the reference-numbered records MC20-004770, MC22-000112, MC23-026854-1 and MC23-028244.

These reference-numbered records should be read together, rather than treated as disconnected correspondence.

The objective is to establish a comprehensible history of what was raised, how it was classified, what was decided and what avenues remained open.

Chapter 6 — Comcare, employment injury and the struggle for recognition

Another substantial documentary cluster concerns Comcare and associated administrative and review processes.

The chronology should establish the relationship between the original employment-related matters, the compensation processes, the medical and assessment material, the administrative decisions and subsequent correspondence.

A compensation file is not merely a collection of forms.

It can contain the dates on which a person’s circumstances were reported, the questions asked by decision-makers, the evidence considered, the operative reasons and the practical consequences of the decisions.

Those details matter.

The declaration asks that the complete sequence be examined with the same care that would be applied to any other substantial administrative history.

Chapter 7 — The NDIS and the question of independence

The NDIS material is particularly important because it concerns the practical circumstances of daily life.

The archive identifies an extensive collection of plan documents, provider correspondence, review decisions and complaints.

These documents relate to different stages and different legal or administrative questions. They must not be collapsed into one undifferentiated finding.

The central human question is much simpler:

What does it mean for a person to have a right to make decisions about their own life if the practical arrangements surrounding their accommodation, support, finances and daily activities leave them feeling unable to exercise that right?

The NDIS was established to support participation, choice, control and independence.

The real-world test of those principles is not the language of a policy document. It is the experience of the person receiving the service.

The record therefore calls for an examination of what support was approved, what was delivered, what was declined, what was reviewed, what changed and how the participant’s own wishes were considered.

Chapter 8 — Able Point: the current chapter

The chronology eventually arrives at the Central Coast and the supported-accommodation arrangements associated with Able Point.

The archive contains a first-person statement dated 14 October 2026 concerning Able Point and NDIS support workers.

The statement records the author’s concerns about support arrangements, the conduct and responsibilities of personnel, incident reporting, safety, independence and the adequacy of services.

It is a first-person record. Its contents should be examined alongside the provider’s contemporaneous records and the relevant agency correspondence.

The documentary questions are substantial:

Who was responsible for coordinating support?

What service agreements were in place?

What was recorded in daily notes?

What incidents were reported?

What action was taken?

What complaints were lodged?

What was communicated to management?

What arrangements were made to protect the participant’s independence and ability to exercise choice?

The archive also identifies a transcript of a call dated 15 April 2026 concerning a reported threat and requests for action.

Its wording, participants, date, context and any available original recording should be examined together.

Another relevant source concerns allegations about support coordination. Any assessment should identify which statements are the author’s allegations, which are supported by correspondence and which require a response from the people or organisations concerned.

The declaration is not a licence to condemn people by association.

It is a demand for the actual records to be examined, the relevant responsibilities identified and any substantiated failures addressed.

The name of a worker, coordinator, executive or public official must never substitute for evidence of that person’s role in a particular event.

Chapter 9 — Crystal: the human being behind the paperwork

There is another subject that runs through the archive and the author’s testimony.

Crystal.

The significance of Crystal is not an administrative statistic. It is part of the human circumstances of the person whose life is being documented.

The author has repeatedly described the importance of his dog, their shared life and his desire to maintain their companionship.

The vehicle decision of 14 November 2025 and the NSW Trustee & Guardian section 122 document of 16 October 2024 should be considered alongside the author’s statements about mobility, accommodation, finances, transport and the practical consequences of the vehicle decision.

The question is not whether an administrative arrangement can be described as technically complete.

The question is whether the decisions made were properly informed by the person’s wishes, circumstances, independence and the practical needs of everyday life.

A person’s attachment to an animal is part of their lived circumstances. It deserves to be considered respectfully.

The declaration therefore makes a simple demand:

Let the record show the person, not merely the paperwork.

PART III — THE WORLD OUTSIDE THE FILE

Chapter 10 — The archive and the public record

A document preserved in a private collection can become part of a much wider public-interest discussion when it is made accessible, organised and connected to the records of the institutions concerned.

That is the purpose of the Barrandodger archive.

The collection is not one document. It is an interconnected body of material containing agency correspondence, administrative decisions, personal statements, research, historical material, legal submissions, registers and supporting files.

The 19 October 2026 finding aid describes a curated collection of more than 2,000 items. The README of the main repository separately records an October 2026 inventory of 5,583 files, including 2,840 PDFs and 1,813 HTML files. Those are different catalogue descriptions and should be understood in their respective contexts.

The archive also identifies an earlier 660-page preservation copy.

The archive’s stated preservation principle is particularly important:

ORIGINAL EVIDENCE MUST REMAIN PRESERVED. WEBSITE PRESENTATION MAY CHANGE. UNDERLYING RECORDS MUST NOT BE ALTERED.

This is a sound documentary principle.

The distinction between preservation and interpretation allows a reader to inspect the source material independently of the narrative built around it.

The repository also identifies OpenTimestamps and SHA-256 integrity information for various records. These mechanisms can assist in verifying file integrity and establishing that particular data existed by a given point in time, where the relevant timestamp proof is independently available and successfully verified.

The archive’s integrity materials also record limitations. For example, the 30 October 2026 consolidated master identifies its own SHA-256 but expressly states that no trusted timestamp, blockchain anchoring or external publication was performed for that particular compilation.

Such distinctions are part of a credible historical record.

The strongest archive is not the one that makes the largest claims. It is the one that allows another person to reproduce the examination.

Chapter 11 — The official government documents register

The Official Government Documents Register is an important component of the wider preservation project.

Its purpose is to organise government and statutory records into an identifiable documentary structure.

The following documents are especially important to the chronology: the Federal Court PID assessment of 27 March 2023; the Ombudsman non-allocation decisions of 9 May 2023 and 24 March 2024; the Ombudsman service restriction of 8 August 2025; the NSW Trustee & Guardian section 122 document; the vehicle disposal decision of 14 November 2025; the NDIS plan approval of November 2025; the AbleCare call transcript of 15 April 2026; the Able Point statement of 14 October 2026; and the legal brief of 23 October 2026.

This is the documentary spine of the declaration.

The reader is invited to move from one source to another, following the chronology and examining the original wording.

The public register is a research instrument, not a substitute for the documents themselves.

Chapter 12 — International human rights: when the question crosses national boundaries

The author’s archive also records an international human-rights communication identified as OHCHR Submission UR/UST/23/AUS/17, and an Australian Human Rights Commission acknowledgment of 4 July 2023.

The distinction between submission, receipt, assessment and outcome must be maintained.

Relevant international instruments include the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, and the Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment.

The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities is particularly relevant to the questions of autonomy, independent living, participation, accessibility and respect for individual choices.

The declaration does not ask the international reader to accept every conclusion in the author’s submissions.

It asks the reader to examine the underlying history through the human-rights principles that Australia has undertaken to uphold.

A democratic system is measured not merely by the rights it declares, but by the practical accessibility of those rights to people who need them.

PART IV — THE NEWSROOM AND THE WIDER PUBLIC INTEREST

Chapter 13 — The story is part of a larger national question

There is a difference between a news report about an individual and a news report about the institutional environment in which that individual is seeking assistance.

No independently identified news article in the material examined establishes that the named agencies or personnel in this archive engaged in a coordinated operation against Dr McLean.

However, significant Australian reporting has documented broader problems involving disability accommodation, NDIS oversight, complaints, safeguarding, restrictive practices and the accessibility of whistleblower protections.

Those articles are relevant background for readers investigating the policy environment. They must not be misrepresented as reporting on this particular case.

The reports named in this declaration include The Guardian of 18 March 2026 on rising notifications from disability group homes; ABC News of 20 January 2026 on a penalty against Oak Tasmania; ABC News of 21 May 2026 on the NDIS integrity inquiry; ABC News of 26 May 2026 on allegations concerning a Broome home; ABC News of 4 April 2025 on a death in supported housing and calls for provider registration; ABC News of 11 October 2025 on a record penalty after a fatal incident; ABC News of 8 October 2025 on an audit of restraints; The Guardian of 9 February 2026 on support cuts; and ABC News of 15 November 2026 on the burden of navigating the NDIS.

These stories are significant because they demonstrate that questions concerning disability safeguards, the quality of supported accommodation, complaints and institutional accountability are matters of genuine national public interest.

They provide a wider context for understanding why a person would want their own support records, decisions and correspondence examined.

They do not establish that the individual events reported by other journalists occurred in this particular case.

The public-interest argument is stronger when that distinction is respected.

Chapter 14 — The right to complain without disappearing

The ABC’s 21 May 2026 report on the NDIS integrity inquiry is particularly relevant to the broader question of whistleblower protection.

The Guardian’s 18 March 2026 investigation provides another important point of comparison: it reports substantial increases in NDIS notifications involving abuse, neglect, injury and restrictive practices, while also recording the Commission’s caution that notifications are not equivalent to established findings.

Together, these articles establish a broader public debate.

The question is not whether every complaint is substantiated.

The question is whether every complaint receives an appropriately accessible, fair and intelligible process.

That is an important distinction for any democracy.

A complaint system that exists only on paper cannot deliver the full protection that its design promises.

A complaint system must be usable by the people it is intended to protect.

A whistleblower must be able to identify the proper recipient, understand the process, receive a meaningful response and know what happens next.

A person with disability must be able to exercise those rights without being overwhelmed by the very procedures intended to provide protection.

The declaration therefore extends beyond the experience of one individual.

It asks whether Australian institutions are prepared to measure their performance by the actual accessibility of their processes.

PART V — THE SCIENCE, THE TECHNOLOGY AND THE DOCUMENTARY BOUNDARIES

Chapter 15 — Patents, surveillance and the difference between capability and deployment

The archive contains a forensic research dossier dated 21 October 2026 concerning patents and technologies associated with human perception, subliminal influence, neuromodulation and radio-frequency auditory effects.

The dossier describes an examination of 163 pages of patent material spanning several decades.

A patent establishes that a technical disclosure was made. It can describe an invention, proposed apparatus or method. Its existence can be investigated through patent databases, filing records, inventors, claims and prosecution history.

A patent’s existence does not establish that the technology was deployed against a particular person.

Likewise, evidence that a technology exists somewhere in the world does not establish that a particular event in an individual’s life was caused by that technology.

The dossier itself acknowledges the need to distinguish disclosure from operational use.

The appropriate declaration is therefore this:

Technology deserves scrutiny. Government powers deserve scrutiny. Human rights must remain protected. And every allegation concerning the use of technology against an individual must be investigated through identifiable, relevant evidence.

Chapter 16 — Artificial intelligence as an independent documentary examiner

The archive is also an example of how technology can be used to organise and examine a complicated documentary history.

Artificial intelligence can help identify titles, dates, reference numbers, missing documents and the difference between a first-person account and an official communication.

But the use of AI creates a responsibility.

It must not invent evidence to complete a narrative.

It must not turn a person’s belief into an official finding.

It must not attribute motives to a named individual without supporting evidence.

It must not create imaginary meetings, hidden files, secret programmes or institutional admissions.

Its task is to make the documentary record more understandable and more accessible.

The most important principle is:

PRESERVATION FIRST. EXAMINATION SECOND. CONCLUSION LAST.

PART VI — THE REVERSAL OF SILENCE

Chapter 17 — They expected the correspondence to end

The correspondence continued.

The documents accumulated.

The administrative reference numbers remained identifiable.

The source repositories became publicly accessible.

The original material was organised into a finding aid.

The chronology was reconstructed.

And the narrative became capable of being examined by people who had never met the author and had no personal involvement in the events.

The significance of survival is therefore not that the institutions have secretly acknowledged a coordinated conspiracy.

It is that the person has continued to preserve and publish the record despite the difficulties described in his testimony.

The archive does not settle every question.

It does make the questions more accessible.

Chapter 18 — The cage of administration

The cage in this story is a literary description of administrative dependency.

It is the experience of being required to navigate a succession of systems in order to obtain decisions about accommodation, support, money, complaints, records and independence.

The institutional system may be divided into separate departments, legislation, contracts and responsibilities.

But the person experiencing the consequences lives one continuous life.

This is the moral question placed before the institutions:

Did the administration of separate responsibilities produce an outcome that adequately respected the person’s dignity, autonomy and welfare?

The answer must come from the records, the people responsible and a fair examination of the decisions.

Chapter 19 — The hunters have not been identified; the questions have

The objective is not to hunt, expose or intimidate individual people.

It is to make institutional decisions examinable.

The presence of a person’s name in a document is not a substitute for establishing what that person did.

The declaration therefore asks:

Who made the decision?

Who authorised it?

Who supplied the information?

Who received the complaint?

Who had responsibility to act?

What was recorded?

What happened afterwards?

And what remedy was available?

That is how accountability should be pursued.

It is also how innocent people are protected from being wrongly accused.

The evidence must lead the narrative, not the other way around.

Chapter 20 — The return of the record

The archive includes the Master Evidence Index of 19 October 2026, the Master Integrity and Source Map of 30 October 2026, the Master Narrative and Registers of 30 October 2026, the legal brief of 23 October 2026 and The Final Reckoning of 24 October 2026.

The reader is not asked to believe that the mere existence of these documents resolves the historical questions.

The reader is invited to inspect them.

The difference between those two approaches is the difference between propaganda and documentary investigation.

The record is strongest when it remains open to examination.

PART VII — THE GREAT RECKONING

Chapter 21 — The institutions must answer the questions that remain

The Great Reckoning is not a prediction of violence, punishment or institutional collapse.

It is the moment when a documentary history is brought together and the organisations involved are invited to account for their respective decisions.

The identifiable subjects are the preservation of government records, the public-interest disclosure processes, the compensation and administrative history, disability support and independence, financial administration, and access to protection and human rights.

They are capable of being investigated through records, decisions, correspondence and responses.

They deserve to be treated seriously.

The declaration does not require a single institution to answer questions outside its jurisdiction.

Chapter 23 — The moral responsibility of public office

The archive includes a particularly forceful statement in the 23 October 2026 legal brief:

Public office is a responsibility to the public, not a sanctuary from accountability.

That principle deserves to stand at the centre of this declaration.

Public officials are human beings. They are entitled to fair treatment, proper process and protection from unsupported accusations.

But public office carries responsibilities.

The moral standard is straightforward:

Treat the person as a person. Examine the evidence. Explain the decision. Correct the error where an error is identified.

PART VIII — THE PROPHETIC DECLARATION

Chapter 24 — The witness remains

Now the language changes.

The preceding chapters have followed documents, dates, correspondence, decisions and public reporting.

The final movement is a declaration of conscience.

The witness remains.

The man who spent years writing letters, compiling records, submitting complaints, requesting assistance and preserving correspondence has not abandoned the work of documenting his experience.

There is no need to invent a secret command centre to understand the significance of a person continuing to document their circumstances.

There is no need to imagine that every official is conspiring to explain the difficulties described in the archive.

The documented history is already substantial enough to warrant serious examination.

The witness remains.

The record remains.

The story goes global.

Chapter 25 — The reversal of power

The most important reversal in this story is not that one person acquires power over another.

It is that a person who feels powerless begins to regain agency through the preservation of his own history.

The individual does not need to control an institution to demand accountability from it.

He does not need to possess public office to ask a public official for reasons.

He does not need a media organisation to authorise the preservation of documents.

He does not need universal agreement with his conclusions to retain the right to speak about his experience.

The archive is an act of preservation.

The declaration is an act of publication.

The investigation is an invitation to scrutiny.

Chapter 26 — The testimony of the forgotten

There are many people whose experiences are difficult to translate into the language of institutions.

The forgotten must not become invisible.

The vulnerable must not become expendable.

The complainant must not become the complaint.

The human being must remain visible behind every file.

PART IX — THE SPIRITUAL DECLARATION

Chapter 27 — Faith is not a finding; conscience is not surrender

The author’s religious language is an important part of the literary and spiritual character of God’s Witness Remains.

It is a declaration of faith.

The biblical tradition contains a recurring theme: the powerful are accountable, the vulnerable deserve protection, and the pursuit of justice requires truth rather than vengeance.

Isaiah 54:17. “No weapon that is formed against thee shall prosper; and every tongue that shall rise against thee in judgment thou shalt condemn.”

Romans 8:31. “If God be for us, who can be against us?”

Psalm 118:17. “I shall not die, but live, and declare the works of the LORD.”

Revelation 12:11. “And they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb, and by the word of their testimony.”

Micah 6:8. “What doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?”

Proverbs 31:8–9. “Open thy mouth for the dumb in the cause of all such as are appointed to destruction. Open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy.”

Psalm 82:3–4. “Defend the poor and fatherless: do justice to the afflicted and needy. Deliver the poor and needy: rid them out of the hand of the wicked.”

The spiritual message is not that another human being must be destroyed.

It is that justice should be pursued without abandoning mercy.

The author’s faith is his own.

The public documentary record is open to everyone.
