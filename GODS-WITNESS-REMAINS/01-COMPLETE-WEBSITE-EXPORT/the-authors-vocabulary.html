The eleven permanent terms

  1. Administrative annihilation. The cumulative effect of ordinary administrative acts — acknowledgements, refusals, non-allocations, declines, disposals, referrals and jurisdictional decisions — where their combined practical consequence may be to remove the means by which a person can live independently, exercise rights, obtain assistance or secure an effective remedy.
  2. Civil death. A living person whose practical means of exercising or enforcing rights have been so diminished that they experience exclusion from ordinary civic life. Not biological death, and not a legal status.
  3. Civil erasure. The person remains named in the paperwork, while housing, autonomy, access, participation or a remedy is not restored.
  4. Civil administrative annihilation. The two faces joined: the administrative acts, and the civil life those acts remove.
  5. Institutional complicity. Participation in, assistance with, or a knowing failure to interrupt harmful conduct where an institution had the knowledge and a responsibility to act. A failure of administration is not automatically a crime.
  6. Conspiracy. In ordinary language, a secret agreement or coordinated action. In criminal law it requires an agreement and an intention that an offence be committed. Parallel decisions are not, by themselves, that agreement.
  7. Police corruption. The abuse of police authority for an improper purpose. It requires the officer, the conduct, the power, the purpose, and the evidence.
  8. Social erasure. The loss of ordinary social standing, recognition, connection and access to a remedy, while the person’s identity remains known.
  9. Social death. Being treated, while still alive, as though one is beyond ordinary social repair.
  10. Societal mobbing. Group isolation, discrediting and exhaustion extending beyond one workplace into the surrounding professional and institutional environment. Separate statutory decisions do not, on their own, prove the group.
  11. Corruption. The use of public power, entrusted authority or public resources for an improper purpose. A harsh decision is not automatically this.

Civil death: the definitions, set side by side

There are three uses of the words. They are not the same thing.

The old legal meaning. In earlier English and European law a person could be civilly dead, civiliter mortuus. Attainder for felony, and in some places entry into a religious order, treated the person as dead in law. Property could pass. The capacity to sue, to contract and to inherit could be lost. That was a status a court or a statute imposed. It has been abolished in Australia. No letter opened for this site imposes it. No opened letter uses the phrase.

The scholarly cousin, which is usually called social death, not civil death. Orlando Patterson, in Slavery and Social Death (1982), used social death for the natal alienation of an enslaved person: denied as a member of a community, not declared dead by a modern Australian statute. Lisa Marie Cacho, in Social Death (2012), writes of law rendering some people ineligible for personhood. Those are arguments about slavery and about criminal law in the United States. They are not findings about these files. They are the books a reader can open if the phrase is to be tested.

The meaning on this site. The civil death page says it is the taking of the ordinary means of enforcing a right, without a charge and without a trial. The person is alive. The right remains written in a statute. The means of using it are what the term says have been removed. The reconstructed master statement says the same thing and adds that it is not a legal status and not biological death.

What the opened letters support is the practical half of that third meaning, and only that half. The estate is committed to the NSW Trustee. The Public Guardian is given residence, health and the lawyers. Services Australia bars the premises and names criminal trespass. The Ombudsman filters the email so it does not arrive. Specialist Disability Accommodation and Supported Independent Living are refused. The plan of 20 November 2025 states $366,406.69 and records no stable accommodation. Those are the instruments. They show a living man separated from money, movement, a home and a remedy by named decisions. They do not show a court declaring him dead in law. The term is his description of that separation. The letters remain referrals, closures, ineligibility, restrictions and disposals.

Bureaucratic violence

Bureaucratic violence is not an offence in the Commonwealth Criminal Code and it is not a term in the NDIS Act. It is a name used by scholars for harm done by ordinary procedure, where no person has to strike a blow.

Johan Galtung, in 1969, called structural violence the harm built into a system when a need goes unmet and no single actor has to intend the injury. Akhil Gupta, in Red Tape (2012), writes of the violence of poverty as it is administered through files, forms and routine writing. Javier Auyero, in Patients of the State (2012), treats the long wait itself as a way a person is dominated. David Graeber, in The Utopia of Rules (2015), describes bureaucracy as a place where violence is structural and rarely named as violence. The common point is the method: the form, the delay, the referral, the eligibility test, the silence.

On this record the method is visible and the intention is not. A Riley apologises for delay and sends the writer to two other offices. The Victorian Ombudsman closes a case because further work is “unlikely to achieve a practical outcome.” Offices decline to allocate a disclosure because he is not treated as a public official, or because they are not the authorised recipient. The Information Commissioner records a deemed refusal, then gives more time. The Ombudsman filters 229 emails. The Trustee disposes of the Ford Territory “to help provide you with enough money to cover budgeted expenses.” Each sentence is an ordinary administrative act. Read together, the acts remove housing, mail, movement, the vehicle and the investigation. That cumulative effect is what the phrase bureaucratic violence is for.

The phrase does not prove a conspiracy, a secret mandate, or a purpose the letters do not write. It is the same distinction already made in the key terms: a consequence, where a purpose is not written. Civil death, in the author’s use, names the condition of the living person. Bureaucratic violence names the method, which is the paperwork. Administrative annihilation is his own name for that method. The letters keep their own names.
