1. The demand: every minister must resign — or acknowledge and investigate

The demand is direct: every minister whose portfolio, decisions, oversight responsibilities or institutional systems are materially implicated must publicly acknowledge the documentary record and ensure an independent investigation. Where that investigation establishes serious misconduct, abuse of office, complicity, retaliation, unlawful decision-making or a grave failure of ministerial responsibility, resignation and any other appropriate consequence should follow. Resignation is not asserted here as a substitute for proof. It is the proposed consequence where responsibility is established.

The central democratic question is not whether one citizen’s interpretation of events must automatically be accepted. It is whether a citizen who has accumulated a large documentary record across public institutions can obtain a reasoned, independent examination of the underlying evidence, rather than being passed from one administrative doorway to another without a determination of the substance. The ministerial appeal itself recognises the necessary distinction between ministerial responsibility, institutional accountability and individual criminal liability. Those questions must be kept separate.

The record raises a testable proposition: over a prolonged period, the author made submissions, complaints, requests for records and demands for protection to numerous agencies; the archive records acknowledgements, decisions, referrals, jurisdictional responses, information-access processes and other administrative outcomes; and the author says that the underlying issues remained unresolved while the practical consequences for his life accumulated. If the original records substantiate that sequence, the result may disclose serious weaknesses in complaint-handling, coordination, safeguarding, procedural fairness or oversight. Whether it also establishes deliberate coordination, retaliation or corruption is a separate question requiring evidence of knowledge, conduct, communication, purpose and causation.

The demand for accountability is therefore not that every institution be presumed guilty. It is that no institution be permitted to treat the existence of another agency, another complaint number or another referral as a complete answer to an issue that remains within its own responsibility. A referral is not an investigation; an acknowledgement is not a finding; a jurisdictional closure is not a substantive answer to facts that were never examined.

9. The recurring administrative pattern: what can be tested

The ministerial appeal describes a recurring cycle: submission of documentary evidence; administrative acknowledgement; referral to another agency; rejection or limitation of complaint scope; failure to examine the underlying allegations; further correspondence; and continued absence of an effective remedy. The 35-Year Testimony similarly states that the author’s central grievance is the repeated receipt of referrals, administrative closures, silence or responses that do not address the substance of what he raised. This is the core analytical proposition of the archive.

To test the proposition, every complaint or disclosure must be coded by issue and outcome. The minimum fields are: date submitted; sender; recipient; reference number; alleged conduct; requested remedy; documents supplied; receipt acknowledged; legal or administrative basis invoked; scope accepted; enquiries actually undertaken; evidence considered; decision-maker; reasons; referral destination; review rights; final outcome; and whether the underlying issue was resolved. The same issue should then be followed across institutions. A referral should be counted as a referral, not as an investigation. A closure should be recorded with its stated basis, not automatically described as proof that no investigation occurred.

A pattern of similar outcomes may be evidence of a systemic problem even where there is no evidence of a coordinated conspiracy. Fragmented jurisdiction, rigid eligibility rules, poor record transfer, repeated requests for the same information, inadequate reasons or failure to identify a lead agency can produce a harmful cycle without central direction. Conversely, evidence of shared instructions, undisclosed communications, common decision-making, inconsistent explanations or retaliatory treatment may support a more serious inference. The forensic report must test both possibilities and actively seek evidence that could disconfirm the author’s preferred explanation.

The strongest formulation supported at this stage is therefore: the archive contains a substantial, multi-agency documentary trail and the author reports that repeated administrative responses have not produced an effective remedy; the pattern warrants independent reconstruction and assessment. The stronger conclusion—that agencies deliberately coordinated to cause detriment—cannot responsibly be stated as established on the present materials alone.

10. The most serious allegations: a separate evidence protocol

The source documents contain allegations of coordinated targeting, fabricated or misleading allegations, surveillance, entrapment, reputational damage, attempted assassination or homicide, concealment and involvement by named public figures or other individuals. These are grave allegations. Their gravity makes disciplined evidence handling more important, not less. They should be set out in a separate schedule so that they do not become conflated with the independently verifiable administrative decisions and correspondence.

For each allegation, the investigator should record the precise event alleged; the author’s first account and date; the identity of any alleged actor; the contemporaneous evidence; independent witnesses; recordings and original metadata; medical or police records; alternative explanations; contradictory evidence; and the exact finding that can properly be made. For surveillance allegations, for example, the existence of a patent, a technical capability, an unusual experience or a perceived pattern does not prove that a device was deployed against the author. For an alleged attempt on life, the relevant evidence would include contemporaneous clinical records, toxicology where applicable, emergency-service records, incident reports, witness evidence and independent medical opinion.

The testimony refers to Tony Ridley, Steve Ison, Bill Shorten and other individuals. It describes recollections, beliefs and allegations concerning relationships, surveillance, entrapment and political or institutional involvement. The present report does not find those allegations proven. It identifies them as matters the author wants examined and specifies the types of evidence needed. Naming a person in a complaint or file title is not proof of that person’s involvement. Any publication of allegations about identifiable individuals must preserve this distinction and avoid presenting suspicion as established fact.

The author’s reported psychiatric history and hospitalisations must also be handled carefully. A psychiatric diagnosis does not establish that a complaint is false; neither does it establish that the complaint is true. Clinical records should be examined for their actual observations, differential diagnoses, dates, treatment decisions and stated basis. The report should not use diagnosis as a proxy for credibility, and should not treat the author’s interpretation of a medical event as a medical finding. Independent clinical review may be required for disputed medical causation or acquired brain injury claims.

The author reports homelessness, poverty, going without food, loss of practical independence, periods living in a vehicle, separation from Crystal, serious health consequences and repeated psychiatric hospitalisations. The ministerial appeal describes difficulties involving accommodation, financial independence, banking, pension access, food, medication, healthcare, legal assistance and disability support. These are reported consequences that can be independently checked through housing records, service-provider files, bank statements, Centrelink records, invoices, medical records and contemporaneous communications.

The humanitarian response should not be made conditional on proving the most serious allegations. Safe accommodation, access to food and medication, appropriate disability support, practical communication, financial transparency and a workable arrangement for the author and Crystal are matters that can be assessed on their present needs. An independent investigation may take months; urgent support decisions should not wait for its final report.

The report should distinguish three causal questions. First, what material hardship or loss of independence did the author experience? Second, which decisions or omissions materially contributed to those outcomes? Third, did any decision-maker act unlawfully, unreasonably, negligently, with improper purpose or in breach of a duty? Evidence may establish the first question without resolving the second or third. A sound report must not collapse the sequence.

12. Cross-agency questions and the required investigation

The investigation should appoint an independent lead with authority to obtain records across the relevant agencies, subject to lawful powers and privacy safeguards. The lead should not be an institution whose conduct is itself under examination. Each agency should nominate a records officer and a decision-maker authorised to answer substantive questions. The process should maintain a master chronology and an issue register so that a matter cannot disappear merely because it is transferred to another body.

The first workstream should authenticate the archive. Preserve each original file, calculate a SHA-256 hash, record file size and format, capture source URL and download date, preserve metadata, identify embedded attachments, and record every duplicate or altered version without deleting it. Where OpenTimestamps receipts are supplied, verify the receipt against the relevant hash and record precisely what the timestamp proves: that a particular hash existed no later than a verifiable time, not that the contents are true or that an agency received them.

The second workstream should reconstruct each agency interaction. Obtain complete incoming and outgoing correspondence, internal file notes, decision records, referral records, case-management entries, search logs, reasons, delegations, risk assessments and records of consultation. Compare the author’s copy with the agency’s copy. Identify missing attachments and any difference between the date sent, date received, date registered and date decided.

The third workstream should examine substantive domains: NDIS decisions and implementation; safeguarding and accommodation; guardianship and financial management; vehicle disposal; hospital treatment and discharge; police threat reports and court proceedings; FOI and privacy searches; whistleblower disclosure pathways; legal assistance; and human-rights complaints. Each domain should have a separate chronology and then be integrated into the master chronology.

The fourth workstream should test cross-agency knowledge and causation. Identify whether one agency informed another, whether information was shared lawfully, whether the same factual claim was repeated without verification, whether a decision relied on another agency’s untested assertion, and whether any conflict of interest or procedural unfairness arose. Similar language is a lead for comparison, not proof of coordination.

13. Proposed findings framework

The final independent report should classify each proposition as: established on the balance of probabilities; established by an authenticated primary record as a discrete event; supported but not yet independently verified; disputed; not established on the available evidence; contradicted by stronger evidence; or outside the investigation’s scope. Criminal allegations should be referred to the appropriate law-enforcement authority and not determined by an administrative report unless the investigator has the relevant legal mandate.

For each established administrative event, the report should state the document identifier, date, author, recipient, decision or action, legal authority, reasons, evidence considered, review pathway, outcome and practical consequence. For each alleged pattern, it should state the number of comparable events, the selection method, the denominator, the time period, the treatment of duplicates, and the alternative explanations considered. This prevents the use of raw document counts as a proxy for proof.

The final report should include an evidence matrix with the following columns: proposition; source document; date; author / institution; original URL or repository path; authentication status; relevant extract; what it establishes; what it does not establish; corroborating material; contradictory material; responsible decision-maker; causal link alleged; alternative explanation; legal or policy issue; status of finding; and outstanding action.
