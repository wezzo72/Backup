Part Four — the meaning of the pattern

13. The Documentary Trap

The phrase The Documentary Trap describes one of the central paradoxes of my experience. Institutions create documents to record their decisions, actions, responsibilities, correspondence and administrative processes. Those documents can later become the very material through which their conduct is examined.

I have developed this argument in The Documentary Trap — When the System’s Own Record Becomes the Evidence.

The argument is not that every official document proves wrongdoing. It is that institutional records can permit independent examination of questions that might otherwise remain disputed. A dated letter can establish that a communication occurred. A formal decision can establish that a decision was made. An acknowledgement can establish that correspondence was received. A service restriction can establish that a restriction was communicated. A refusal can establish the outcome of a particular process.

When these records are arranged chronologically, it becomes possible to ask whether decisions were consistent, whether relevant information was considered, whether explanations were adequate and whether the cumulative consequences were addressed. This is why preservation matters.

A person may be disbelieved. A complaint may be rejected. An allegation may be disputed. But the existence and wording of an original document can still be examined. The archive therefore invites an investigation that begins with the source material, not with a predetermined conclusion.

Social erasure is the term I use for the progressive loss of the practical conditions that enable a person to participate in society. It is not limited to reputation. It can involve the loss of financial independence, a secure home, employment, communication, relationships, mobility, legal assistance, the ability to have complaints heard, control over ordinary decisions, and recognition as a person whose testimony deserves consideration.

These conditions are interconnected. A person who cannot access money may struggle to obtain transport. A person without transport may struggle to attend appointments. A person without reliable communication may struggle to contact services. A person without appropriate legal assistance may struggle to challenge decisions. A person whose accommodation is unstable may find it harder to maintain employment, relationships or health routines. 16. Institutional fragmentation and the disappearance of responsibility

One of the most troubling features of complex administrative systems is the possibility that responsibility becomes fragmented. One organisation may deal with accommodation. Another may deal with funding. Another may deal with guardianship. Another may deal with complaints. Another may deal with privacy. Another may deal with legal assistance. Another may deal with policing. Each may have a defined jurisdiction.

That division of responsibility can be legitimate and necessary. But it can also create a practical problem when the person affected cannot obtain an answer about the cumulative consequences of the decisions.

Who is responsible for understanding the whole situation? Who ensures that one decision does not create a problem that another organisation then refuses to address? Who takes responsibility for correcting information that has been misunderstood? Who ensures that a person is not left without a practical route to assistance simply because their circumstances cross multiple administrative boundaries?

I have described this as a form of institutional abandonment.
