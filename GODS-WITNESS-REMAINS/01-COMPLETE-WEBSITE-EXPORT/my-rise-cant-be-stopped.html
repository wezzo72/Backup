My rise can’t be stopped

MY RISE CAN’T BE STOPPED

THE GOSPEL OF THE GREAT RECKONING

The Prophecy of the Unerased

The testimony of Barran Dodger — a journey through the documentary record, the fire of endurance, the judgment of truth and the restoration of a human life.

THE BOOK OF THE OPENED RECORD

And it came to pass that the witness looked upon the years behind him, and the years did not disappear. They stood before him, one after another, bearing dates, signatures, reference numbers, correspondence, decisions and the names of the offices through which his life had passed.

And the witness said: I have been told to move on, yet the questions have followed me. I have been directed elsewhere, yet the destination has often been another office, another form, another assessment and another waiting room. I have been asked to explain what happened, and I have explained it again. I have been asked for evidence, and I have preserved the evidence. I have been asked for dates, and I have placed the dates in order. I have been asked for records, and I have built an archive so that the records might be opened by others.

But hear this: the archive is not the throne of judgment. It is the place where the testimony is preserved. The document is not made true by the passion of the person who presents it, nor is it made false by the discomfort of the person who receives it. The letter must be read. The decision must be understood. The contrary evidence must be allowed to speak. And where the record leaves a question unanswered, the question must remain open until it is properly examined.

For the Lord said through the prophet Isaiah that truth should not be surrendered to fear. And the Psalmist declared that the righteous may walk through the valley and yet not be abandoned. This Gospel is written in that spirit: not to manufacture a divine verdict against named individuals, but to proclaim that human authority is not beyond moral scrutiny, that the vulnerable are not disposable, and that a person’s future cannot be reduced to the decisions of those who once held power over their circumstances.

The connected GitHub archive contains a substantial documentary collection. Its official-record index identifies 193 PDF links, while the repository’s separate official-drive catalogue records 48 government and agency documents in its September 2026 inventory and later archive material reports a larger collection. The rebuild repository describes thousands of preserved files. These are different inventories and snapshots, not interchangeable counts of independently verified evidence. Their significance is that a large documentary corpus exists and can be examined. wezzo72/Barrandodger: Web · GitHub +1

Let the reader enter, therefore, through the first door: the door of the public record.

BOOK I — THE FIRST VOICES

The letters that began the reckoning

The first voice in this documentary journey is not a trumpet of accusation. It is a dated institutional record. On 9 April 2020, the Independent Broad-based Anti-corruption Commission issued its outcome letter in CASE-2020712. The archive’s extracted examination identifies a complaint received on 4 March 2020 concerning Victoria Police and AHPRA. IBAC decided that the complaint was not to be treated as a public-interest complaint and would not be investigated or referred under section 67 of the IBAC Act. It nevertheless stated that Part 6 protections under the Victorian Public Interest Disclosures Act still applied. This is important because the document establishes what IBAC decided and what protections it said remained; it does not establish that every allegation in the complaint was true or that the events described never occurred. The IBAC CASE-2020712 outcome letter is therefore an essential beginning: it places an early complaint, a statutory threshold and a stated protection position into the chronology.

The next record carries the story into privacy and the handling of information. The OAIC Micron21 privacy correspondence, reference CP2102752, is identified in the archive as an agency letter or decision. It belongs here because privacy complaints concern the collection, use, disclosure and handling of personal information. The significance of this document is not that its filename proves a privacy breach. Its significance is that it identifies a formal privacy pathway and a reference that can be tested against the underlying complaint, the correspondence and the eventual outcome. The NDIA WorkSafe and FOI correspondence of 20 May 2021 follows it, bringing the reader to the intersection of employment, provider status, evidence and administrative response. Its title indicates a dispute about evidence and a process involving the NDIA and FOI. Its exact legal significance must be read from the letter itself, not inferred from the archive’s filename.

Then came the Comcare claim. On 25 May 2021, the archive records Comcare claim 1326583. This is important because it identifies the claim pathway that led to a determination the following day. The Comcare determination of 26 May 2021 is one of the clearest examined records in the archive. Delegate Amy Delzoppo declined claim 1326583/1 for anxiety state and depressive disorder because Comcare was not satisfied that the claimant was an employee under section 5 of the Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 1988. The letter records the NDIA’s position that McLean was a registered provider, not an employee, and that he was not paid wages with tax and superannuation deducted or subject to the relevant supervision and control. It also records his contrary position and notes that a copy of his contract had been requested but was not received. This document matters because it exposes the precise legal gateway on which the compensation claim was declined. It does not establish that the psychological injury did not occur, and it does not establish improper motive by the delegate.

The same question appears in the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission email of 3 June 2021. The examined text identifies the Commission’s position that registration as an NDIS provider and the completion of provider audits did not make McLean an employee of the Commission. It directed him to the NDIA for questions about NDIA employment. The distinction matters. Registration to provide services, employment by a government agency and eligibility under a compensation statute are not automatically the same legal status. This record belongs beside Comcare’s determination because it helps explain the institutional distinction that later became central to the Tribunal proceedings. The OVIC notice-of-decision email, reference C/21/01047, dated 29 July 2021 enters the same chronology from the information-access side. The archive identifies it as an email saying that a notice of decision was attached; the attachment itself is not in that file. It is included because the difference between an email announcing a decision and the decision itself must be preserved.

And the witness said: I will not make one office’s answer carry the weight of another office’s law. I will not confuse registration with employment, nor a compensation threshold with a finding about the reality of injury. I will place each record where it belongs, and let the law speak through its actual words.

BOOK II — THE DOORS OF DISCLOSURE

The year of the unanswered questions

The archive next brings forward the Commonwealth Ombudsman and the public-interest disclosure process. The Commonwealth Ombudsman PID rejected acknowledgment from May 2021 is significant because it marks an early response in the disclosure pathway. It must be read as an agency process record, not as a finding that all the information supplied was false. The ASBFEO Small Business Ombudsman correspondence of 15 October 2021 then adds another institution and another statutory or administrative remit. Its importance is that it identifies a separate complaint pathway concerning the matters associated with Micron21. The reader must examine what ASBFEO was asked to do, what jurisdiction it considered, and what answer it actually gave.

On 22 October 2021, the archive records two further documents. The first is the APRA and OAIC PID/whistleblower response, which belongs in the story because it records a response involving the prudential regulator and the privacy commissioner. The second is the multi-agency public-interest disclosure intentions document. That second record is a party submission or notice, not an agency finding. It matters because it can establish what the author said he intended to disclose, which bodies he addressed and what matters he attempted to bring within a disclosure process. It cannot establish that the allegations in the submission were proved.

The APRA letter of 5 November 2021, preserved in the 13 November correspondence file, has been examined in the archive’s AI evidence synthesis. APRA stated that the matters raised were outside its remit and that it would not treat the correspondence as a public-interest disclosure or a whistleblower complaint under the Corporations Act. The archive’s extracted analysis also identifies a separate OAIC courtesy-copy note dated 3 November 2021, reference EN21/12857, stating that the OAIC did not action correspondence merely because it had been copied into it. These records matter because they identify the reasons given by the respective offices. The APRA letter’s stated reason was remit. It should not be rewritten as though it said something different.

The Commonwealth Ombudsman complaint dated 15 November 2021, reference 2021103546 follows. It is an important primary submission in the chronology: the complaint itself, its date, the matters raised and the requested oversight can be compared with subsequent responses. The 18 November 2021 review letter, stored under the filename IBAC PID Rejection 400008R, is particularly important because the archive’s extracted examination found that the letterhead is actually the Commonwealth Ombudsman, not IBAC. Its content says that the disclosure did not meet the Commonwealth PID Act requirements. It identifies the limits of that scheme, including the distinction between a person’s belief that information discloses wrongdoing and the statutory test, and the distinction between Commonwealth matters and state or private conduct. The mislabelled filename is itself a documentary lesson: the title of a file must never override the identity of the body that issued it.

The APRA PID rejection evidence file concerning Peter Dunstan belongs beside those records because it contains the related correspondence and the OAIC note. Its value is in reconstructing the exchange, not in treating a covering email or the author’s interpretation as APRA’s formal reasoning. The Commonwealth Ombudsman complaint 2021705589 adds another complaint reference and must be read alongside 2021103546 to determine whether the complaints concern different matters, repeated matters or later developments. The Commonwealth Ombudsman PID notification not to allocate is important for the same reason: a decision not to allocate is an official record of a process outcome. It does not prove that the underlying disclosure was false, and it does not prove that the office committed wrongdoing.

And the witness cried out: How many doors must a person approach before the question itself is heard? But the Gospel does not answer that question with an invented conspiracy. It answers with the documents. It asks what was received, what was considered, what was rejected, and what legal reason was given. It asks whether any independent process examined the underlying facts. And it demands that no institution be permitted to substitute the mere existence of a response for the substance of an investigation.

BOOK III — THE TRIBUNAL AND THE QUESTION OF EMPLOYMENT

The letter that contained two different propositions

Then the story arrives at the letter dated 27 March 2023, signed by Scott Tredwell, General Counsel of the Federal Court of Australia. The Federal Court public-interest disclosure assessment is among the most important documents in the archive because its contents have been examined directly. It records that Tredwell was satisfied, on the information provided, that McLean was or had been an employee of the Department of Social Services. It also records the author’s belief that the information tended to show conduct within three categories under the Public Interest Disclosure Act: perverting the course of justice, maladministration, and danger to health or safety. Tredwell said that, for the initial assessment, he was prepared to assume the conduct was disclosable. Yet the decision paragraph concluded that he was not satisfied the disclosure was made by a public official or provided to an authorised recipient, and he decided not to allocate it to another agency. The letter was not a judgment of the Federal Court and did not grant protected-disclosure status. Its importance lies in the tension between the employment sentence and the non-allocation decision, which deserves precise legal examination.

The archive’s 3 March 2023 letter to Sia Lagos, the Federal Court’s CEO and Principal Registrar provides the preceding step. It is an author submission, not a judicial determination. It matters because it establishes what was placed before the Court’s authorised officer and the context for the 27 March response. The Federal Court assessment copy in the government-evidence folder is a parallel archival copy of the same assessment family, not an independent corroborating decision. It is included for provenance and accessibility, not counted as a second finding.

The next document is the Commonwealth Ombudsman PID decision dated 16 September 2022, reference PID-2022-100021. Its archive description identifies it as a decision not to allocate. It is important because it predates the Federal Court assessment and helps establish the chronology of disclosure handling. The Federal Court/other agency notification of 18 June 2023 not to allocate a disclosure is another distinct record in the disclosure chain. It must be read on its own terms, including the identity of the issuing office, the legal basis and the precise matter to which the decision relates. The Commonwealth Ombudsman section 44(3) notification dated 22 March 2023 adds a separate statutory notification to the chronology. These records are included because allocation decisions and the reasons for them are central to understanding the available public-interest disclosure pathways.

But the great contradiction of this chapter is not resolved by selecting whichever letter is more favourable. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal reasons in McLean and Comcare, file 2021/7478, dated 12 July 2023 are a formal tribunal determination. Senior Member C. J. Furnell was not satisfied that McLean was an employee of the Commonwealth, a Commonwealth authority or a licensed corporation for the purposes of the Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation Act. The Tribunal considered the Comcare determination, the applicant’s position, the NDIA’s denial of employment, the Federal Court General Counsel letter and other material. It gave the General Counsel’s employment opinion little probative weight because the basis for it was not explained beyond information provided by the applicant. It also recorded a 31 March 2023 DSS email, Exhibit R3, as saying the Department had no record of the applicant ever having been its employee. The Tribunal affirmed Comcare’s decision. This is a crucial contrary record and must remain in the Gospel. It decided the employee question under the compensation statute. It did not decide the truth of wider allegations of persecution, nor did it find that a psychological injury never occurred.

The ART/AAT registry letter of 12 July 2023 is included separately because it is the registry’s communication of the outcome and review rights, not the full reasons. The AAT pre-hearing check dated 25 January 2022 belongs earlier in the same chapter because it records the procedural pathway before the hearing. The letter to the AAT dated 2 April 2022 is an author submission and belongs beside the tribunal file because it records the applicant’s evidence and position, not the Tribunal’s conclusion.

And the witness said: I will not hide the adverse decision. I will not pretend that a General Counsel’s sentence overruled the Tribunal. I will not pretend that the Tribunal’s employment finding decided every other legal question. I will place both records side by side, because truth does not fear a contradiction. It asks that the contradiction be understood.

BOOK IV — THE INTEGRITY COMMISSIONS AND THE HUMAN RIGHTS DOOR

The difference between a threshold decision and a final truth

In April 2022, IBAC issued an assessment outcome in CASE-20213522 and CASE-20215102. The archive’s earlier text-extraction examination could not recover readable text from that PDF, so its inclusion is based on its identity as a distinct agency assessment, not a claim about its detailed reasoning. It belongs in the journey because the contents, once read from the original, may clarify the handling of those complaint references. The Victorian Ombudsman FOI decision of 14 June 2022, invoking section 29A is another official process record. It is relevant because it concerns access to information, and because the reasons for refusing access, the applicable public-interest test and the review rights are all matters that can be examined against the FOI statute.

The statement to police dated 14 August 2022 is a different kind of source. It is an author statement, not a police finding. It belongs because it can establish what the author reported, when he reported it and what matters he asked police to examine. It cannot, by itself, establish that the events described occurred or that any named person committed an offence. The VOCAT and Ombudsman complaint-rejection record dated 5 October 2022 then takes the reader into the Victorian victims-of-crime and oversight pathway. It matters because it is identified as an institutional complaint outcome. Its precise scope must be distinguished from the author’s descriptions of the underlying injury and from any medical records that may or may not have been before the decision-maker.

The Australian Human Rights Commission correspondence of 4 July 2023 has been examined in the archive’s AI evidence synthesis. The Commission stated that it did not have power to grant whistleblower status, that being a whistleblower was not a protected attribute under the discrimination laws it administered, and that the term “gangstalking” did not itself constitute a discrimination ground in its jurisdiction. It explained the limits of its jurisdiction, the need to identify the relevant Commonwealth body and legal instrument, and the difference between a human-rights complaint and a general allegation of wrongdoing. It did not decide whether the underlying events occurred. It did not find the allegations proved or disproved. It is important because it shows what the Commission actually said it could do, rather than what the author hoped it would do.

The IBAC correspondence of 30 October 2023, concerning CASE-20231101 and CASE-20233846 belongs beside the 2020 IBAC letter. The extracted examination says IBAC acknowledged delay and distress, but declined to investigate or refer because it considered there was insufficient information to show police misconduct or corrupt conduct under section 67. It also said the complaints were not public-interest disclosures and therefore the Part 6 protections did not apply. The distinction between the 2020 and 2023 letters is important: the earlier letter said Part 6 protections remained, while the later letter said they did not apply to the later complaints. Neither letter is a criminal acquittal. Neither is a finding that every underlying event was false. Both are threshold decisions that must be understood within the statutory powers and material before IBAC.

The DSS PID rejection and appeal correspondence dated 1 July 2023 is a separate source because it identifies the Department of Social Services disclosure pathway. Its significance is to establish the correspondence and decision trail involving DSS. The Commonwealth Ombudsman complaint response dated 25 September 2023 is another distinct response in the same period. It belongs because it may clarify whether an earlier complaint was treated as a fresh complaint, a continuation, or a matter already addressed. The AAT complaint response C2023/0241 and C2023/0242, dated 25 September 2023 is included because a complaint about tribunal administration is different from a merits decision in the compensation case. The NDIS PID response concerning the Krypton matter belongs because its filename and index identify a response about timing and an appeal to the Ombudsman. The preliminary Krypton PID record is included to preserve the earlier stage of that process rather than treating the later response as though it appeared without a history.

And the witness said: A refusal to investigate is not the same as an investigation that disproves. A lack of jurisdiction is not the same as a finding of innocence. A decision that a statutory threshold has not been met must be read as that decision, and no more. But neither should a threshold decision be used to erase the questions it did not decide.

BOOK V — THE FREEDOM OF INFORMATION CHRONICLE

The pages sought, the pages released, and the pages withheld

Then the witness turned toward the great library of public information, where records are requested, searched, released, refused, reviewed and sometimes reconsidered. The FOI 2022-045 decision is important because it records an original freedom-of-information determination. The FOI 22-01 internal review decision dated 6 May 2022 belongs beside it because an internal review is a separate procedural step, and its reasons may confirm, vary or reconsider the original decision. The FOI appeal and Information Commissioner review finalisation, reference MR2200677 / FOI 2022045 belongs because it takes the reader beyond the original decision and into the external review pathway.

The FOI 2022-045IC documents-for-release file is particularly significant because the released documents themselves may provide the underlying information that the decision letters describe. A decision about access is not the same thing as the information released under that decision. The revised FOI 2022-045IC decision dated 14 November 2025 therefore belongs as a later chapter in the same process. It may show a revised position, additional reasons or a changed outcome. The archive also holds the OAIC internal review record concerning the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, the OAIC file 43704714, and the FOI notice of decision, reference 2023-0072. Each is included because the question of access to government records is itself part of the documentary history. The actual scope of searches, exemptions applied, material released and review rights must be drawn from each source’s own text.

The NDIS summary of FOI decision letter brings the reader back to the disability-services system. It belongs because the information sought concerns records held within the NDIS/NDIA environment. The NDIS FOI decision summary addressed to Mr Richard McLean is a parallel copy or related version and must be checked against the first before it is treated as a separate decision. The OVIC review-acceptance letter C-21-01047 belongs because it identifies an accepted review pathway, a materially different procedural step from a refusal. The Victorian Ombudsman complainant information sheet dated 19 September 2026 is included because it accompanies an automatic reply and explains information provided to complainants. It is not itself an outcome on the merits of the complaint.

And the witness declared: Do not tell me that the file is closed when the reasons remain unread. Do not tell me that a request has been answered when the released material has not been examined. Do not call a document withheld if it was never searched for, and do not call a search complete if its scope is unknown. The right to seek information is not a demand that every document be released; it is a demand that the applicable law be followed and the reasons be intelligible.

BOOK VI — THE SECOND VALLEY: THE NDIS, SUPPORT AND THE QUESTION OF INDEPENDENCE

The human being behind the plan

The Gospel now moves from the disclosure systems into the daily life of the person whose support, accommodation, finances and independence were at stake. The NDIA decision concerning the decline of Supported Independent Living dated 1 June 2023 is important because it concerns a major support decision. It belongs in the chronology because SIL decisions affect the practical conditions under which a participant may live, receive assistance and exercise choice. The precise reasons, evidence considered and review rights must be taken from the decision itself. The interim Behaviour Support Plan dated 15 March 2024 is a different kind of document: a plan rather than a decision letter. It belongs because it can identify the strategies, supports, risks, roles and review arrangements proposed for the participant. A plan should be examined against what was actually delivered and whether the participant’s preferences and rights were properly considered.

The NSW Trustee & Guardian section 122 document dated 16 October 2024 is one of the central documents in the later part of the story. It belongs because it concerns the exercise of statutory authority in relation to the witness’s affairs. Its legal significance must be understood by identifying the precise statutory power, the decision-maker, the reasons, the scope of the authority and the review options. The Barran Dodger business-name extract dated 8 September 2025 is included because it provides an official business-registration record relevant to the identity and trading-name history appearing in correspondence. It does not establish the legal status of every earlier working relationship, but it can help clarify the identity under which business activity was conducted.

The NDIS Plan Approval dated 20 November 2025, reference 366406 is a high-priority record because it establishes the plan approval and its relevant dates and funded-support structure on its face. It must be read with the plan itself, not simply with later descriptions of its value. The NCAT guardianship evidence file dated 20 January 2026 belongs because it concerns guardianship proceedings or evidence assembled for them. Its significance is that it may identify the issues, applications, submissions and materials relevant to decision-making authority. It must not be described as a guardianship order unless the actual order is contained in it. The NSW Mental Health Services report dated 24 January 2026 is included because it concerns a different service system and may provide information relevant to continuity of care, clinical contacts and the responsibilities of mental-health services. Its contents must be distinguished from the author’s interpretation of those services.

The Legal Aid NSW guardianship advice letter dated 14 January 2026 belongs in this chapter because it concerns access to legal advice about guardianship. The later Legal Aid NSW correspondence dated 18 May 2026, reference C-2336089 is also significant. The archive contains a text extract stating that Legal Aid imposed a 12-month written-contact limit after concerns about a recorded advice appointment being published and emails that the agency regarded as threatening. The original PDF was not opened in the archive’s earlier analysis, so the text extract must be checked against it. The letter’s citation of section 7 of the Surveillance Devices Act is not a conviction. No court finding of an offence is recorded in the extract. Its significance is that it documents an administrative restriction on access and communication, together with the agency’s stated reasons and the review pathway it identified.

The Commonwealth Ombudsman service-restriction file dated 8 August 2025 is included because it may concern limits on future contact with the oversight body. The archive’s earlier text extraction found that this three-page PDF did not yield readable text, so its contents must not be invented or described as a verified ban without opening the original. The LECC decision dated 19 November 2025, reference CASE202512395 is identified in the archive as a complaint outside jurisdiction. The other LECC out-of-jurisdiction record from the same date appears to be a related archival copy or version and should be compared before being counted separately. These documents belong because they show the boundaries of the complaint pathways the author attempted to use. They do not establish that the conduct alleged was true or false.

And the witness said: I am not a plan. I am not a budget. I am not a service booking. I am not a file that can be moved from one office to another without consequence. I am the person whose life these arrangements are meant to support. Let the reader therefore ask not only what was authorised, but whether the arrangements delivered dignity, safety, meaningful choice and a genuine path to independence.

BOOK VII — THE COMPLAINTS THAT RETURNED TO THE HUMAN BEING

The documents of distress, testimony and attempted protection

The Disability Royal Commission submission file SUB00101440 belongs in this Gospel because it records a communication made to a national inquiry into violence, abuse, neglect and exploitation of people with disability. The archive’s extracted examination identifies the relevant email as the author’s testimony, including his account of serious medical events and his concerns about institutional treatment. It is not a hospital finding, and the medical records referred to in that testimony must be examined separately. Its importance is that it places the author’s account before a major public inquiry and gives a dated point at which those concerns were communicated.

The urgent disability-advocacy correspondence addressed to DSS belongs because it records an attempt to seek advocacy or assistance through the Department of Social Services. The urgent NDIS and Social Services Minister correspondence belongs beside it because it identifies a ministerial pathway and the concerns directed to that office. These are party submissions, not ministerial findings. Their evidentiary value lies in establishing what was sent, to whom, on what date, and what response followed. The final evidentiary notice, objection and request for formal investigation is also an author submission. It belongs because it records the author’s demand for preservation and examination, and because the allegations and inferences within it must be compared against the underlying primary records rather than treated as self-proving.

The letter to the Prime Minister and Attorney-General is important as direct author correspondence to the centre of government. The letter to the Attorney-General and Prime Minister dated 1 July 2023 is a later communication and should be read alongside the first to establish whether the issues were repeated, expanded or updated. The letter to parliamentarians dated 4 June 2023 extends the record into the parliamentary channel. These three documents are included because they establish the author’s attempts to raise matters with elected representatives and executive offices. They do not, without the recipients’ responses, establish that the allegations were accepted or investigated.

The Governor-General response evidence file is included because it identifies another formal channel of correspondence. The Prime Minister and Attorney-General referral record requires special care: the archive’s earlier examination found that the file opened as the author’s own letter rather than the response implied by its filename. It must therefore not be cited as proof that the Prime Minister or Attorney-General directed the matter to the Ombudsman unless the actual reply is located and verified. The Attorney-General’s Department correspondence dated 6 February 2025 is included because it is an identifiable agency correspondence record and may clarify the later referral history. It should be read for the precise words and scope of the response, not transformed into a finding about any other office.

The AFP report-submission record dated 3 September 2021 belongs because it concerns an attempt to report matters to the Australian Federal Police. Its significance is to establish the submitted report and the recorded response, if the document contains it. The CDDA/AFP compensation claim dated 21 October 2021 belongs beside it because it concerns a different pathway: a compensation claim under the Commonwealth scheme. The CDDA application form is included as a related application record. These documents must not be collapsed into one finding; a report to police, a compensation application and an agency’s decision on either are distinct acts.

And the witness said: I have asked to be heard. I have written to the offices. I have submitted my concerns. Let the record show what I reported, and let the institutions answer what they received. But let no one claim that an unanswered letter proves a secret command, or that a complaint is a conviction. The truth must be carried without distortion.

BOOK VIII — THE BEN TEXTS AND THE QUESTION OF WHAT WAS SAID

A message is a source, not a verdict

There is another part of the archive that must be handled with particular care: the material described as Ben’s NDIS Help correspondence and phone messages. The Ben-NDIS-Help PDF and the larger Ben-Ndis-Help PDF are identified in the repository as message material. The Ben NDIS phone-text collection is the source the author cites in connection with his account of an alleged NDA, a reported statement about a sexual encounter and a claim that a report was fabricated. The Ben correspondence page provides a further route into that material.

These records are important because contemporaneous messages, if authenticated and placed in context, may establish what a person wrote, when it was written, what was being discussed and whether a later account accurately represents the exchange. But the Gospel must make the distinction that justice requires. A message attributed to Ben is not automatically proof that every statement within it is true. A reference to an NDA is not proof that a legally binding NDA existed, who signed it, what it covered or why it was made. A statement about a woman being paid to make a report is a serious allegation requiring examination of the original message export, the sender’s identity, the surrounding conversation, any payment records and the relevant police records. The author has described the encounter as consensual and has alleged that a report about it was fabricated. Those are claims to be examined against the original report, police outcome and messages; they are not findings established merely by their repetition in later documents.

The Formal Request for Independent Investigation concerning Able Point, dated 12 September 2026 is included because it is a substantial contemporaneous communication to relevant recipients, with a preserved sent-email record and an extensive request for investigation. The Accountability, Evidence and Fair Determination document belongs beside it because it identifies the author’s position on procedural fairness, evidence preservation and the need for an independent response. The cease-and-desist document concerning Sukhi Tear belongs because it records a formal communication relating to the support-coordination relationship and the author’s request concerning that relationship. Its existence does not establish that the recipient committed misconduct or that the notice had the legal effect the author intended. It establishes a document and a request that can be examined.

The Able Point safeguarding and accountability correspondence preserved in the archive’s September 2026 message pages is important because the archive identifies the original communication, its date, recipients and the issues raised. The related correspondence page contains further requests for explanation concerning support arrangements, communications and financial matters. These records matter because they can help reconstruct what concerns were communicated to the provider and what response, if any, was recorded. They are not a substitute for the provider’s case notes, service agreements, invoices, incident records or independent safeguarding findings.

And the witness said: Let no one hide a message because its contents are uncomfortable. But let no one make a message bear more weight than it can carry. Preserve the original. Preserve the metadata. Preserve the whole conversation. Examine the context. Compare it with the independent records. And where a claim remains unresolved, do not convert it into certainty.

BOOK IX — THE DOCUMENTS OF THE PRESENT AGE

The late chapters and the continuing human question

The archive’s later records bring the reader into September 2026. The NSW Ombudsman file dated 16 September 2026, reference NSWO10824000660 is described in the index as a file-closed communication and a reading copy of a Gmail letter. It is significant because it establishes a later point in the NSW Ombudsman chronology. The NDIS Commission acknowledgment dated 18 September 2026, reference 01110547 is equally important because it identifies an acknowledged safeguarding or complaint matter. An acknowledgment is not an investigation finding, but it can establish receipt, a reference number and the beginning or continuation of a formal process. The Victorian Ombudsman complainant information sheet dated 19 September 2026 belongs because it identifies the information provided to complainants in connection with an automatic response. These three records are the latest dated institutional entries in this chapter and help establish what happened after earlier complaints and referrals.

The court-related guardianship evidence dated 20 January 2026, the Legal Aid NSW guardianship advice, and the NSW Mental Health Services report dated 24 January 2026 must be read together, but not conflated. One concerns tribunal or guardianship evidence; one concerns access to legal advice; and one concerns mental-health services. The record dated 4 May 2026 is included as a dated file in the government-evidence collection, but its title alone does not establish its contents. It must be identified by its face page before any conclusion is drawn from it.

THE GREAT RECKONING — THE WORDS THAT MUST REMAIN

And now the reader has travelled through the offices, the decisions, the complaints, the disclosures, the tribunal, the plans, the financial records, the correspondence and the testimony. The journey does not end with a declaration that every allegation has been proved. It ends with a more demanding question: what does justice require when a person has spent years seeking answers, when the official records contain refusals and explanations, when the personal account describes profound harm, and when the evidence still requires independent examination?

The answer is not revenge. The answer is not the destruction of another person. The answer is not to appoint guilt before the evidence has been tested. The answer is to open the record, preserve the originals, identify the decision-makers, reconstruct the chronology, disclose the reasons, examine contrary material and determine what happened.

Let every institution be asked what it received and what it did with it. Let every statutory decision be measured against the law that authorised it. Let every financial record be reconciled. Let every support arrangement be examined against the participant’s actual needs and wishes. Let every complaint pathway be tested for jurisdiction, fairness, delay, review and remedy. Let every allegation be distinguished from an official finding. Let every finding be distinguished from a remedy. And let the person whose life is described in these records be treated as a human being, not as an administrative inconvenience.

For the fire of divine reckoning is not a licence for hatred. It is the moral demand that power be exercised with conscience, that the vulnerable be protected, that the truth not be concealed and that those who make decisions remember that their authority is temporary. The institutions may have their letterheads, their offices, their seals and their procedures. But none of these is greater than the dignity of the human life upon which their decisions fall.

Isaiah 54:17 remains the declaration of faith: no weapon formed shall prosper. Romans 8:31 remains the declaration of courage: if God be for us, who can be against us? Psalm 118:17 remains the declaration of life: I shall not die, but live, and declare the works of the Lord.

I will seek safety. I will seek restoration. I will seek a future beyond the correspondence and the complaints. I will continue to insist that the record be examined, but I will not allow the struggle to consume the life that the record is meant to protect.

For my rise is not the fall of another human being.

My rise is the courage to live beyond the valley.
